Design Yelp

Proximity search and business reviews — the canonical "find things near me" problem.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Yelp is the textbook geospatial-search interview. The whole question lives or dies on one decision: how do you index millions of businesses by location so a "near me" query is fast? At L6/L7 the interviewer wants to see you reach for a deliberate geo-index — geohash or quadtree — and defend the radius/precision tradeoff, then quietly precompute rating aggregates instead of scanning reviews on every read. Get those two right and the rest is plumbing.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “Do searches combine text, category and distance, or distance only?” — Combined queries push toward Elasticsearch rather than a pure geo index.
  • “How fresh must ratings be after a new review?” — Seconds of staleness lets us update averages asynchronously.
  • “Is the search radius fixed or user-chosen?” — Variable radius affects geohash precision and neighbor-cell queries.

Functional Requirements

Three things a user must be able to do. Everything else is out of scope.[1]

#Requirement
1Search for businesses near a location (by name and/or category), e.g. "coffee within 2 km of here".
2View a business — its details plus its reviews and average rating.
3Leave a review: a mandatory 1–5 star rating and optional text.

Out of scope (call these out, then drop them): admin/business-owner management, rendering a map UI, personalized recommendations, photos, spam/fraud detection, GDPR.[1]

Phase 2 · 5–10 minEstimate what mattersOnly the numbers that change a decision — say the implication out loud.

Capacity Estimation

Only estimate the numbers that change a decision. Three matter here.

  • Businesses: ~10M total. At ~1 KB metadata each that's ~10 GB — fits comfortably in a single database, and the entire geo-index easily fits in memory. This is the key insight: the data is small. The hard part is the access pattern, not the volume.
  • Reviews: say 10M businesses × ~50 reviews avg ≈ 500M rows, growing. At ~1 KB each ≈ 500 GB — large enough to want sharding by business_id, but not enormous.
  • Search QPS: 100M DAU × ~10 searches/day ≈ 1B/day ≈ ~12K QPS average, peaking maybe 3–5×. Reviews written are orders of magnitude fewer (~hundreds/sec). Implication: read path must be cheap and cacheable; write path can be asynchronous.

Interviewer pushback

“If the data fits in memory, why not scan it?”

Staff answer: A scan of 10M rows per query at thousands of QPS blows the 500 ms budget. A geo index narrows candidates to nearby cells first.

Say to move on → “10M businesses is ~10 GB, so the geo index fits in memory. The problem is the access pattern. Let me define the API.”

Check before continuing

If the data fits in memory, why not just scan it on every query?

Fitting in memory doesn't make a linear scan fast enough at 12K+ QPS; a geo index narrows candidates first.

Reread the pushback right after the capacity estimation box.

The capacity estimate says search QPS (~12K) vastly outnumbers review-write QPS. What design choice does that difference justify?

A workload dominated by reads justifies spending engineering effort on caching reads, while rare writes can afford to be async.

Data Model

Postgres is the source of truth; Elasticsearch and Redis are derived. The two modeling choices that matter: store rating_sum and review_count (not just the average) so updates are exact integer increments, and put a UNIQUE (business_id, user_id) constraint on reviews so one user can't inflate a rating by reviewing twice.

High-Level Design

Start with the dumb version that works, then attack the bottleneck. Requests enter through an API gateway / load balancer and fan out to stateless services.

ComponentRole
Search ServiceHandles GET /search: queries the geo-index for candidate businesses in the radius, filters by category/term, ranks, returns.
Business ServiceHandles business reads/writes and review writes.
Business DBPrimary store for businesses + reviews (e.g. PostgreSQL or DynamoDB). Reviews sharded by business_id.
Geo-IndexThe location index — geohash/quadtree (Deep Dive 1). Maps a region → set of business ids.
CacheRedis in front of hot business pages and popular searches (Deep Dive 3).

Search flow: request → Search Service → compute the geo-cells covering (lat, long, radius) → look up candidate business ids → fetch their rows (which already carry avg_rating) → filter/rank → respond.

Review-write flow: POST /reviews → Business Service writes the review row → emits an event so the average rating and search index update asynchronously (Deep Dive 2). The user gets a fast 201; consistency catches up in seconds.[2]

Staff answer: Search needs geo, text and category ranking together at high QPS; isolating it lets us use Elasticsearch and scale reads independently of review writes.

Phase 5 · 27–47 minDeep dives & tradeoffsGo deep where the system breaks; compare alternatives and justify the pick.

Deep Dives

Deep Dive 1 — Geospatial search: how to index by location

This is the problem. We have 10M businesses with (lat, long) and a query "give me everything within radius R of point P, fast." Walk the alternatives explicitly — the interviewer is grading your judgement on the tradeoff, not the final keyword.

ApproachIdeaVerdict
Naive box scanIndex lat and long separately (B-trees); query WHERE lat BETWEEN … AND long BETWEEN ….✗ Two independent ranges; DB can only use one index well, scans a huge stripe. Falls over at scale. Mention it only to reject it.
GeohashInterleave lat/long bits into one Z-order string; nearby points share a prefix. Prefix length = precision = cell size.[3]✓ Compact string, trivially indexable in any DB or Redis (GEOSEARCH), prefix lookups are fast. The pragmatic default.
QuadtreeRecursively split space into 4 quadrants until each cell holds ≤ K points. Dense cities → deep tree; empty ocean → shallow.[4]✓ Adapts to density — no wasted depth in sparse areas. In-memory structure; rebuild/update cost is the catch.
PostGIS / ElasticsearchManaged spatial index — PostGIS uses R-trees; Elasticsearch geo_point uses a BKD tree.[5]✓ Don't build it yourself if a store already does it. ES also gives you text + category in the same query (Deep Dive 4).
★ Staff-level signal Pick deliberately and own the precision/radius tradeoff. The recommended pick is geohash for its simplicity, compactness, and clean fit with a distributed cache — but the signal is why, expressed through precision. A geohash precision (prefix length) maps to a cell size: 5 chars ≈ 5 km cell, 6 chars ≈ 1.2 km, 7 chars ≈ 150 m. Choose the precision whose cell is roughly the query radius, then query the matching cell plus its 8 neighbors to cover points that fall just across a boundary, and finally re-rank candidates by exact Haversine distance.[3] If the interviewer pushes on dense areas (a geohash cell in Manhattan holds far more businesses than one in Wyoming), that's your cue to compare a quadtree, which splits by density so no single cell blows up. Naming geohash and articulating the cell-size lever is what separates a staff answer from "I'd use a geo-index."

Deep Dive 2 — Computing & serving the average rating

The naive read path: on every business view, SELECT AVG(rating) FROM reviews WHERE business_id = ?. For a business with 10K reviews, served millions of times, you re-scan the same rows endlessly. That's the trap.

Compute on readPrecompute on write (pick this)
HowAggregate reviews at query time.Store avg_rating + review_count on the Business row; update on each new review.
Read costO(reviews) scan every time. Bad for read-heavy.O(1) — the value is already there.
Write costFree.One extra update per review (rare event).

We are read-heavy and reviews are rare, so precompute on write is the clear winner. The update is incremental, not a re-scan: new_avg = (avg×count + new_rating) / (count+1), then count++. Do this update asynchronously off an event emitted by the write — the writer doesn't block, and eventual consistency is acceptable per our NFRs.[2]

Keeping it consistent. The risk is a lost or double-applied update under concurrent reviews. Two clean fixes: (a) do the incremental update inside an atomic transaction / conditional write on the Business row so concurrent reviews serialize; or (b) treat the review stream as the source of truth and have a single consumer fold ratings per business (events keyed by business_id so one business is processed in order). Don't hand-wave "I'll just update the average" — name the race and how you close it.

Deep Dive 3 — Scaling reads: caching popular searches & pages

At ~12K+ search QPS the access pattern is heavily skewed: a handful of cities, categories, and famous businesses dominate. Two cache layers:

  • Business-page cache: Redis keyed by business_id → serialized detail + avg_rating. Popular pages never touch the DB. Invalidate (or short TTL) when a new review lands.
  • Search-result cache: key on the normalized query — rounded (geohash-cell, category, radius-bucket) — so "coffee near downtown" hits one entry instead of millions of slightly-different lat/longs. A short TTL (seconds–minutes) keeps it fresh enough given eventual consistency.

Rounding the geo key to a cell is the trick that makes search caching actually work — raw float coordinates are never identical, so they'd never hit. Add read replicas behind the DB for the long tail that misses cache.

Deep Dive 4 — Search by name + category combined with geo

"Pizza" is a text/category match; "near me" is a geo filter; the interviewer wants both in one query, ranked. Stitching a geo-index to a separate keyword index and intersecting in app code is painful. The clean answer: Elasticsearch, which combines an inverted index for text/category with a geo_point index (BKD tree) in a single bool query — text match filtered by a geo-distance clause, scored by relevance and distance together.[5]

Keeping ES in sync. Elasticsearch is a derived read store, not the source of truth. The Business DB stays authoritative; changes (new business, edited category, updated avg_rating) flow to ES via the same async event stream / CDC used in Deep Dive 2. If ES is briefly stale, eventual consistency covers us. State plainly that ES serves reads and the DB owns writes — that division is the staff move.

Interviewer pushback

“Geohash, quadtree or PostGIS?”

Staff answer: Geohash for simplicity and cache-friendly keys, querying the cell plus neighbors to fix edge effects; Elasticsearch geo_distance when text and category are combined. A quadtree only pays off with very uneven density.

Say to move on → “Production readiness next.”

Check before continuing

In a geohash index, what does increasing the prefix length (precision) do?

More prefix chars = finer precision = smaller cell. You match precision to the query radius.

Reread the staff-signal callout in Deep Dive 1.

Why is a naive "index lat and long separately" box scan rejected?

Two independent range conditions can't both use an index efficiently; the DB scans a large stripe.

Reread the "Naive box scan" row in Deep Dive 1's table.

Why does the design query the matching geohash cell plus its 8 neighbors, instead of just the matching cell?

Geohash cells are a grid; a point just across the query point's cell edge is geographically close but hashes to a different cell.

Reread the staff-signal callout's sentence on querying "the matching cell plus its 8 neighbors."

Privacy

PII: reviewer identity, review text (can reveal where someone lives, eats, or seeks medical care), and search locations. Location minimization: the query location is used in-flight; logs and caches keep only a coarse geohash; no location history is stored. Public vs private: reviews are public by design — display a first name + initial by default. Deletion: account erasure deletes or anonymizes reviews and decrements rating_sum/count via the same event path so aggregates stay accurate. Retention: search logs aggregated after 30 days. (The lesson scoped GDPR out of the interview, but a real launch still needs these controls.)

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Yelp is a read-heavy proximity search over a small dataset. Businesses live in Postgres and are indexed in Elasticsearch for combined geo, text and category queries under 500 ms. Hot searches and pages are cached. Reviews are written to the source of truth, and an asynchronous consumer folds each rating into a running sum and count, so average ratings are O(1) and seconds stale. Geohash precision sets the tradeoff between radius and candidate count. At 10× we add replicas and regional shards.

Comprehensive check

Why precompute avg_rating on write instead of computing it on read?

Reads vastly outnumber writes; storing the aggregate turns every read into O(1) and pushes cost to rare writes.

Reread Deep Dive 2's compute-on-read vs precompute-on-write table.

Which store cleanly combines a text/category match with a geo-distance filter in one query?

Elasticsearch joins an inverted index (text/category) with a geo_point BKD index in one bool query.

Reread Deep Dive 4.

Search-cache keys round the query location to a geohash cell. What would break if the cache instead keyed on raw lat/long floats?

Raw floats basically never repeat exactly, so a cache keyed on them never gets hits; rounding to a cell makes repeat queries collide.

Reread the sentence "Rounding the geo key to a cell is the trick..." in Deep Dive 3.

Elasticsearch is kept in sync with Postgres via async CDC rather than a synchronous dual write. What does this trade away, and why is it acceptable?

Async CDC means search results can briefly lag the database, which the NFRs explicitly allow in exchange for a simple, decoupled write path.
