System Design · Lesson 29 · Phase 4 — Case Study · Hard

Design Uber (Ride-Sharing)

Match a rider to a nearby driver in under a minute, never double-book a car, and survive a firehose of GPS pings.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Uber is the canonical geospatial-plus-matching interview. The breadth (fare → request → match → trip) is easy to sketch; the L6/L7 signal is entirely in three deep dives — a geospatial index that answers "nearest drivers" in milliseconds, a matching step modeled as an atomic claim so two riders never grab the same car, and a write firehose you keep out of your durable database. Get those three right and the rest is plumbing.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “Do we need fare estimates before requesting, and surge pricing?” — Estimates drive routing load; surge adds a pricing loop.
  • “How long can a driver take to accept an offer?” — The offer timeout becomes the lease TTL for matching.
  • “Is pooling or batched dispatch in scope?” — Batched matching changes the matcher from per-request to a periodic solve.

Functional Requirements

Scope ruthlessly. Three flows carry the whole interview [1]; payments, ratings, and pooling are explicit non-goals you name and defer.

#Riders / drivers should be able to…
1Enter pickup + destination and get a fare estimate (price, ETA), then request a ride at that fare.
2Be matched to a nearby available driver, who can accept or decline the offer.
3See each other's live location on a map from match through pickup and drop-off.

Capacity Estimation

Only compute the numbers that change a decision. The one that dominates is the location-write rate; the rest size the tiers around it.

Data Model

Data is classified by durability. Durable facts (riders, drivers, fares, rides) live in a relational store partitioned by city; ephemeral state (positions, offer leases, socket routing) lives in Redis with TTLs and is regenerated within seconds. A partial unique index on rides(driver_id) for active statuses backs up the Redis lease: even if a lease misfires, the database refuses a second active ride for the same driver.

Deep Dive 1 — Tracking & querying driver locations

"Find the nearest available drivers" is a 2-D range query. A B-tree index on lat and lng separately is useless — it can't prune in two dimensions at once, so you fall back to scanning a bounding box. The fix is a spatial index that maps 2-D into a sortable 1-D key. (See Lesson 116 — Geospatial Indexes for the mechanics.)

ApproachIdeaVerdict
Naive DB + B-treeStore every ping in a table, query a lat/lng box.✗ No true 2-D pruning; firehose writes are ruinously expensive [1].
PostGIS / quadtreeSpatial DB with quadtree partitioning; batch pings before persisting.~ Real 2-D queries, but batching = stale positions; still durable-write load.
Redis GEO (in-memory)GEOADD encodes lat/lng as a geohash stored as a sorted-set score; GEOSEARCH returns drivers within radius.✓ Sub-ms queries, handles the write rate, TTL auto-expires stale drivers [1].

How the encoding works: a geohash interleaves the bits of latitude and longitude so that points close in 2-D space share a long common prefix — turning a 2-D range search into a 1-D prefix/range scan over a sorted set. Quadtrees and Google's S2 cells solve the same problem with adaptive cell sizes (better for wildly uneven density, e.g. Manhattan vs. a rural highway). For an interview, geohash-via-Redis is the clean default; mention S2/quadtree as the alternative when density is skewed.

Only index available drivers. On match, you remove the driver from the index; on trip-end you re-add. The index stays small and every result is actually offerable.

★ Staff-level signal Don't reach for a geo database by reflex. The staff move is recognizing that the query is "k nearest available drivers right now," that positions are disposable, and that an in-memory geohash index satisfies both the latency NFR and the write rate — while a durable spatial DB satisfies neither cheaply. Naming the geohash↔sorted-set mapping (and S2 as the skewed-density alternative) shows you understand why it's fast, not just that Redis has a GEO command.

Deep Dive 2 — Matching as an atomic claim (no double-assignment)

This is the consistency NFR, and the single most common place candidates leak race conditions. Multiple Matching Service instances may pull overlapping nearby-driver sets and offer the same driver to two riders. You must model the offer as an atomic claim, not a read-then-write. (This is the contention/locking pattern from Lesson 10 and Lesson 118.)

ApproachMechanismVerdict
In-process lockLock the driverId inside one service instance.✗ No coordination across instances; lock lost on crash [1].
DB status columnSet driver status = offered; a cron releases stale ones.~ Works, but timeouts ride on a cleanup job; couples matching to the DB.
Redis lock + TTLAtomic SET driver:{id} rideId NX EX 10 — only one offer wins; key auto-expires in 10s.✓ Single winner, self-healing timeout, no cron [1].

The flow: Matching Service picks the best nearby driver and attempts an atomic SET ... NX EX 10. The NX (set-if-not-exists) makes the claim a compare-and-set — exactly one instance can acquire it, so a driver is offered to at most one ride at a time. Then push the offer.

Offer timeout & fallback: the 10-second TTL is the offer window. If the driver accepts, write driverId onto the Ride durably and confirm. If they decline or the TTL expires, the lock vanishes and the Matching Service moves to the next candidate. Because the lock is ephemeral, a crashed instance can't strand a driver — the worst case is a 10-second wait.

Sequencing offers durably. "Offer driver 1, wait 10s, fall to driver 2, …" is a small state machine that must survive crashes. A naive in-memory loop or a pile of delay-queue messages gets fragile fast (hard to cancel when the driver accepts mid-window). A durable workflow engine (Temporal / Step Functions) models the whole matching attempt as a persistent workflow with built-in timeouts and resume-after-crash [1] — the staff-grade answer when dropped matches mean lost revenue.

★ Staff-level signal Senior candidates say "use a lock." Staff candidates frame the requirement precisely: a match is a compare-and-set on driver availability with a self-expiring lease, where the lease TTL is the offer timeout — collapsing two problems (mutual exclusion and timeout/fallback) into one primitive. Knowing the lock should be ephemeral (so a crash degrades to a short delay, not a stuck driver) is the judgment that separates a robust design from one that wedges under failure.

Deep Dive 3 — Live trip location streaming

Once matched, both phones need each other's position updating every few seconds — a server-push problem. Polling wastes battery and adds latency; the right primitive is a persistent connection. (See Lesson 2 — Networking and Lesson 2 — Real-Time Delivery.)

OptionFit for live trip tracking
Short polling✗ Per-request overhead, laggy, drains battery at a few-second cadence.
SSE (server→client only)~ Push works, but a trip is bidirectional (both sides move and send).
WebSocket✓ Full-duplex, low overhead, long-lived — ideal for both parties streaming positions.

The Connection Service holds the rider and driver sockets keyed by rideId. The driver's location ping enters via the Location Service; for an active trip it's also relayed to the rider's socket (and vice-versa for the rider's position to the driver). Scale the connection tier independently — it's a fan-out/relay layer, stateless apart from the socket map (which can live in Redis so any node can route). On disconnect, the client reconnects and resumes; brief gaps are acceptable, so this path favors availability over perfect ordering.

Deep Dive 4 — Taming the location-write firehose

Two million writes per second is the number that breaks naive designs. The discipline: never let a raw ping touch a durable, indexed database — it's expensive, it's pointless (a position is stale in seconds), and it would bottleneck the very store your rides depend on.

Layered defenses against the firehose:
  • Adaptive client cadence. A parked driver pings rarely; a fast-moving one pings often. Pushing this logic to the app cuts the write rate at the source [1] — the cheapest write is the one never sent.
  • In-memory landing zone. Pings update Redis GEO directly (overwrite, not append) with a short TTL. The "current position" is just the latest value; history isn't kept.
  • Lightweight transport. A position update doesn't need a full TLS REST round-trip; a slim binary/UDP-style channel reduces per-ping cost.
  • Geo-sharded ingestion. Shard the Location Service and geo index by region so each shard absorbs only its city's writes — a per-city spike stays local [1].
  • Async analytics tap (optional). If you do want trip history for billing/ML, fork a copy to Kafka and batch it to cold storage — off the hot path, never blocking the index.

Durability of the index. "But Redis is in-memory!" — yes, and that's fine here. Positions are reconstructed within seconds: every driver re-pings on the next cadence. Back it with RDB/AOF persistence and Sentinel/replica failover for fast recovery, and accept that a few seconds of lost positions self-heal. Contrast with the Ride record, which is durable and consistent. Knowing which data deserves durability (rides) and which doesn't (positions) is the whole game.

★ Staff-level signal The firehose answer is a data-classification argument, not a technology list. State it as a principle — "ephemeral, regenerable data stays in memory; durable, queryable facts go to the DB" — then show you'd shed load at every layer (client cadence → transport → in-memory overwrite → geo-shard) before scaling any store. Pairing that with "I'd tap a copy to Kafka only if billing/ML needs history" shows you separate the hot path from analytics instead of overbuilding.

Privacy

Ride data is precise location over time for both riders and drivers — home, work, and every trip. Minimization: raw pings aren't persisted on the hot path; if archived, they're stored per trip, not per person over the day, and coarsened after the billing/dispute window (e.g. 90 days). Exposure: riders see the driver's location only between match and drop-off; drivers see pickup/drop-off, not the rider's live location after the trip. Masking: phone numbers proxied through a calling service. Employee access: location lookups require a ticket justification and are audited (a real historic failure mode in the industry). Deletion: account erasure anonymizes rides kept for tax/regulatory retention.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Uber splits a location firehose from a correctness-critical match path. Driver pings (~2M/s) update an in-memory geo index sharded by city and never touch a durable table. Ride requests query nearby available drivers, rank them by ETA from a self-hosted routing engine, and offer rides via an atomic lease whose TTL is the offer timeout, so a driver can't be double-assigned. Durable workflows track the ride state machine, and matched parties stream positions over WebSockets. At 10× we split cities into sub-regions and add nodes.

Comprehensive check

Why is a plain B-tree index on latitude and longitude a poor fit for "nearest driver" queries?

A 1-D B-tree can't co-prune lat and lng; geohash/quadtree/S2 map 2-D into one sortable key.

How does an atomic SET driver:{id} NX EX 10 claim solve two problems at once?

NX gives mutual exclusion (single offer); the TTL doubles as the offer timeout and self-heals on crash.

Why is it acceptable to keep live driver positions only in an in-memory store?

Positions are ephemeral and regenerable; durable consistency is reserved for the Ride record.
