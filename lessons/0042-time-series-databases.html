Time Series Databases

A store built for one shape of data — append-only, time-ordered samples — where the workload is write-dominated and the real enemy is cardinality, not QPS.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Metrics, IoT, financial ticks, app telemetry — all are write-heavy time-series workloads, and the lazy answer ("put it in Postgres") collapses under the write rate and series count. The staff move is to reach for a purpose-built TSDB, explain why a row store fails, and name tag cardinality — not raw throughput — as the central scaling problem. This generalizes the engine you built in Lesson 38.[1]

1 · What makes time-series data special

A time-series point is metric name + tags + value + timestamp — e.g. http.latency{service=checkout,region=us} 142 @t. The workload has a distinctive shape that a TSDB exploits:[2]

PropertyConsequence for the engine
Write-dominated — millions of samples/sec, append-onlyOptimize for sequential appends, not random updates → B-trees lose.[1]
Time-ordered & immutable — points rarely change once writtenPartition by time; old data is read-only and compressible.
Range-scan reads — "p99 latency over 2 weeks"Columnar layout + pre-aggregation; never scan raw points.
Recent data is hot, old data is coldTiered storage + downsampling by age.

2 · The storage engine — LSM blocks + WAL

Almost every TSDB (Prometheus, InfluxDB's TSM) uses an LSM-style block store.[1] Writes hit a Write-Ahead Log for durability, accumulate in an in-memory head block, then flush to immutable, time-partitioned blocks on disk; background compaction merges small blocks into larger ones.[1] Prometheus crystallized the canonical layout: 2-hour head blocks, 128 MB WAL segments, compacted upward (6h → 18h → 54h) up to 31 days or 10% of retention.[4]

This is the LSM-tree from Lesson 03 specialized for time: append-only writes, immutable sorted blocks, and a read that touches only the blocks overlapping [from, to].

3 · Compression — 16 bytes → ~1.3 bytes/sample

A naïve (timestamp, value) pair is 16 bytes. TSDBs crush that ~10× with two tricks borrowed from Facebook's Gorilla:[4]

  • Delta-of-delta on timestamps. Samples arrive at near-fixed intervals, so the change in the gap is usually 0 — stored in a bit or two.[2]
  • XOR on float values. Consecutive values are similar; XOR them and store only the differing bits (leading/trailing zero counts).[4]
Result: ~1.3 bytes per sample on typical telemetry — the economics that make the whole system affordable.[4]

4 · Cardinality — the real scaling constraint

This is the heart of the topic. A series = one unique (metric name, label set) combination; the TSDB indexes by series, so the unit of cost is the number of distinct series, called cardinality.[5] One unbounded label — user_id, request_id, raw URL — can multiply series count by a million overnight.[2]

The cost is brutal: the in-memory head holds one chunk per active series, so a million series costs ~2–4 GiB of RAM on Prometheus, and ~10 million is the practical ceiling for a single instance.[3] Defenses: drop high-cardinality labels (move IDs to logs, not tags), relabel/aggregate at the source, and refuse cardinality bombs at the door rather than silently OOM-ing.[2] This — not QPS — is what breaks the system.

5 · Downsampling & retention tiers

Raw, high-resolution data is expensive and mostly useless after a few days — so downsampling isn't optional, it's the economic foundation of long retention.[2] A background compactor rolls raw points into pre-aggregated, coarser blocks, and old tiers expire:

Typical tiering: raw 15 days → 1-minute rollups 90 days → 1-hour rollups 2 years. A query auto-selects the cheapest resolution that still answers it. Going 5s → 1h resolution cuts row count ~720×.[1] A two-week chart reads pre-aggregated hourly blocks, so query cost scales with plotted points, not raw ingest.

Senior candidates scale QPS; staff candidates scale series and know when not to add a second datastore.[1]

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. Why does a B-tree relational store struggle with metrics ingest?

The workload is write-heavy and append-only; B-tree random index writes can't keep up — TSDBs append sequentially instead.

2. How does a TSDB compress 16-byte points to ~1.3 bytes each?

Gorilla-style encoding: near-constant intervals make timestamp delta-of-deltas tiny, and XOR of similar floats stores few bits.

3. What is the primary scaling constraint of a time-series database?

Cost scales with distinct series (name × label combos). One unbounded label can explode cardinality and OOM the head.

4. Why does downsampling make multi-year retention affordable?

Coarser rollups (raw→1m→1h) shrink old data ~720× at 5s→1h, so long-range queries read cheap pre-aggregated blocks.
