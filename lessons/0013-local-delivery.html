Design a Local Delivery Service (Gopuff)

Browse is a read-heavy proximity-plus-inventory join; checkout is a strongly-consistent decrement. Two paths, two consistency choices.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Gopuff looks like a simple e-commerce CRUD problem, and that is the trap. The L6/L7 signal here is recognizing that availability browse and order checkout have opposite requirements — one wants fast, slightly-stale, fan-out reads; the other wants serializable correctness so you never sell the same physical can of Cheetos twice. Picking different consistency models for the two paths is the whole game.[1]

1 of 8

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “How fresh does browse availability need to be?” — If a minute of staleness is fine, browse can be cached; checkout still must be exact.
  • “Can one order be fulfilled from multiple centers?” — It changes whether checkout reserves across DCs in one transaction.
  • “What is the delivery radius, and does it depend on road travel time?” — Travel time vs straight-line distance decides whether we need a routing engine.

Functional Requirements

Gopuff delivers convenience-store goods from 500+ micro-fulfillment centers (we'll call them distribution centers, DCs) within roughly an hour.[2] Two operations dominate; everything else is out of scope.

#Requirement
1A customer can query item availability deliverable within ~1hr near their location. Availability is the union of inventory across all nearby DCs.[1]
2A customer can place an order for multiple items at once without double-booking the same physical units.[1]
3The order is fulfilled (delivered) within the 1-hour window the availability promised.

Explicitly out of scope: payments, driver routing and dispatch, search/ranking, cancellations and returns.[1] Stating scope crisply is itself a senior signal — it stops you boiling the ocean.

Non-Functional Requirements

PropertyTarget / priority
Availability-read latency<100ms — browse feels like search; users won't wait.[1]
Freshness of availabilitySeconds-to-a-minute stale is acceptable on browse; perfect freshness is not required.
Consistency at checkoutStrong — two customers must never buy the same physical inventory.[1]
Scale~10k DCs, ~100k catalog items, ~10M orders/day.[1]
Read >> writeReads (browse) dwarf writes (orders) by orders of magnitude — design for the read path first.

Notice the deliberate split: availability tolerates staleness (you can show a stale "in stock" and reconcile at checkout), but checkout cannot tolerate overselling. That asymmetry justifies different storage and consistency choices per path.

Say to move on → “Browse needs to be fast and a bit stale; checkout must never oversell. Let me size both.”

Phase 2 · 5–10 minEstimate what mattersOnly the numbers that change a decision — say the implication out loud.

Capacity Estimation

Only estimate numbers that change the design. The headline derivation:

Availability QPS. 10M orders/day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ ~115 orders/sec. But browse fans out: assume ~10 availability checks per purchase and a ~5% conversion rate. That inflates reads to roughly ~20k availability queries/sec.[1]
  • Implication of 20k read QPS: a single primary can't serve this. You need read replicas and/or a cache in front — the read path must scale independently of writes.
  • Implication of ~115 write QPS: orders are low volume. A single strongly-consistent primary (e.g. Postgres) can comfortably absorb writes, so you can afford expensive serializable transactions on the order path.
  • Implication of 100k items × 10k DCs: the inventory table is ~1B rows worst case — small enough for one well-partitioned database, not "needs a special datastore" territory.

The numbers themselves tell the story: read-heavy browse, light-but-correct writes. That is the entire architectural thesis.

Interviewer pushback

“Why not compute nearby DCs with a live routing call on every browse?”

Staff answer: At 20k QPS, per-request routing is slow and expensive. Precompute DC reachability per geohash cell and refresh periodically, so browse is a cached lookup.

Say to move on → “Browse is ~20k/s and orders ~115/s, so reads and writes get separate paths in the API.”

Check before continuing

Roughly how do browse QPS and order QPS compare?

~20k browse QPS versus ~115 order QPS — the fan-out (10 checks per purchase, 5% conversion) inflates reads far past writes.

Reread the capacity box's two bolded QPS numbers.

Why does the pushback answer precompute DC reachability per geohash cell instead of routing live on every browse call?

Precomputing turns an expensive per-request routing RPC into a cached lookup, which is the only way the read path survives 20k QPS.

Data Model

One relational database, partitioned by region (zip3) so a location-scoped browse or order touches one or two partitions. The load-bearing constraint is CHECK (qty >= 0) on inventory: even a buggy caller cannot oversell, because the database refuses the decrement.

High-Level Design

Two services, split along the consistency seam.

ComponentResponsibilityData store
Availability ServiceResolves nearby DCs, sums their inventory for the requested items, returns the union.Inventory DB read replicas + cache.[1]
Nearby ServiceMaps a (lat,long) to the set of DCs that can deliver within 1hr.In-memory DC table, refreshed periodically.[1]
Orders ServiceRuns the strongly-consistent reserve-and-record transaction.Inventory DB leader (Postgres).[1]

Browse flow: GET /v1/items → Availability Service asks Nearby Service for DCs → reads their inventory from a replica (cache-first) → sums and returns. Order flow: POST /v1/orders → Orders Service opens one serializable transaction against the leader → checks + decrements + records → commit-or-abort. The replica/leader split is what lets browse scale to 20k QPS while orders stay correct.

Phase 5 · 27–47 minDeep dives & tradeoffsGo deep where the system breaks; compare alternatives and justify the pick.

Deep Dives

1. Computing availability by location

Availability = "which DCs can reach me?" joined with "what do those DCs hold?". The naive move — scan all 10k DCs and compute distance per request — is too slow at 20k QPS. Hello Interview's refined approach is a two-phase prune:[1]

Phase A — coarse filter (cheap, in-memory). Periodically (e.g. every 5 minutes) sync the DC table from the database into the service's memory. On each request, apply a hard radius cutoff — about a 60-mile "most optimistic drive distance" — to throw away the vast majority of DCs with simple math.[1]

Phase B — precise filter (expensive, external). For the handful of survivors, call the Travel-Time Service to confirm true drive time ≤ 1hr. Pruning first means you make a few RPC calls, not thousands.[1]

Then aggregate: query Inventory + Item for the surviving DCs, sum the quantities, and return the union to the client.[1]

Precompute vs. query-time. You could precompute a DC-set per geohash/zip and store it, refreshing as DCs open/close — great for read latency, but the precompute is stale and the join to live inventory still happens at read time. Query-time geo (the prune above) keeps the DC mapping fresh and is cheap once the coarse filter does its job. The pragmatic answer: precompute the slow-changing geometry (DC → coverage area), compute the fast-changing inventory sum live. Geospatial indexes (geohash, quadtree, PostGIS) are the standard tool if you push Phase A into the database instead of memory.

2. Keeping availability reads fast and fresh

At 20k QPS, summing inventory across DCs on every request hammers the database. Two complementary moves:[1]

TechniqueHowCost
Redis cache, short TTLKey on (location-bucket, filters, page). Hit → return. Miss → DB lookup, then write back. TTL ~1 minute bounds staleness.[1]Cache invalidation: the Orders Service should evict affected entries on write.
Read replicasBrowse reads hit replicas; orders write the leader. Distributes the 20k QPS off the write path.[1]Replication lag → browse can show slightly stale stock.

This is where the read/write tradeoff becomes a feature, not a bug. A 1-minute-stale "in stock" is fine because checkout re-validates against the leader. The cache is allowed to lie a little; the transaction in deep dive 3 is the safety net. That layering — eventually-consistent browse fronting a strongly-consistent buy — is the staff-level insight the problem is testing.

3. Inventory consistency at checkout (no overselling)

The hard part. Two customers click "buy" on the last unit at the same DC in the same millisecond. Exactly one must win. The clean solution is a single atomic transaction on the leader:[1]

One SERIALIZABLE transaction:
  • Check every requested item has quantity > 0 at a reachable DC.
  • If any item is unavailable → abort the whole order.
  • Otherwise atomically: record the Order and flip that Inventory from available → ordered.
  • Commit or fail as a unit.[1]
Under serializable isolation, the two concurrent transactions conflict; one commits, the other fails to commit because the inventory is no longer available and is cleanly rejected.[1]

Alternatives and the tradeoff:

MechanismHow it prevents oversellWhen
Serializable transactionDB serializes conflicting orders; loser aborts.[1]Default pick — simplest, correct, low write volume affords it.
Atomic decrementUPDATE … SET qty=qty-1 WHERE qty>0; check rows-affected.Counter-style stock; avoids full serializable cost.
Optimistic concurrencyVersion column; retry on version mismatch.Low contention, want high throughput.
Reservation / holdReserve units with a TTL at "add to cart", confirm at pay.Long checkouts; protects scarce stock from cart-hoarding.

The stated tradeoff of the all-or-nothing transaction: simplicity and ACID guarantees, at the cost of flexibility — if one item goes out of stock mid-order the entire order fails rather than partially fulfilling.[1] Naming that tradeoff out loud is the senior-to-staff move.

4. Scaling reads

Cache + replicas handle volume; partitioning handles the per-query scope. Partition the inventory data by region — e.g. the first 3 digits of the zipcode — so a location-scoped availability query touches "mostly 1 or 2 partitions rather than the entire inventory dataset."[1] Combined with replicas per partition, reads scale horizontally by region while writes still funnel to that region's leader.

The full read-scaling stack, cheapest layer first:
  • Redis cache (1-min TTL) absorbs the hottest repeated queries.[1]
  • Read replicas spread surviving reads off the write leader.[1]
  • Regional partitioning shrinks each query's footprint to 1–2 partitions.[1]
★ Staff-level signal A senior candidate builds two services and scales the reads. A staff candidate names the seam: availability is a read-heavy proximity-plus-inventory join that should be precomputed and cached with relaxed freshness, while checkout is a write that demands serializable correctness — so they get different consistency models on purpose. The cache is permitted to be stale precisely because the order transaction re-validates against the leader and aborts cleanly on conflict. If you can articulate why eventual consistency on browse and strong consistency on buy compose safely, you've shown the judgment the level requires.[1]

Privacy

PII: name, phone, delivery address and precise lat/lng, order history (which can reveal health or lifestyle — e.g. pharmacy items). Minimization: browse only needs a coarse geohash, so round coordinates before they hit logs or cache keys; store precise location only on the order. Retention: precise delivery coordinates purged or coarsened 90 days after delivery; order records kept for tax/accounting with PII fields separable. Deletion: erasure request anonymizes orders.user_id and address fields while keeping financial totals. Access: DC staff see only the fields needed to pick and hand off an order.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Local delivery splits along a consistency seam. Browse availability is a read-heavy join of nearby DCs and inventory, precomputed per geohash cell and cached with a minute of staleness to serve ~20k QPS. Checkout is a low-volume (~115/s), strongly consistent transaction that conditionally decrements inventory on the primary, so we never oversell. Partitioning by region keeps transactions local, and at 10× we add regional leaders and cache shards without changing the design. I'd next add delivery slot capacity and driver assignment.
