Design Instagram

A photo-sharing feed at billion-user scale — where blob storage meets fan-out.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Instagram is two hard systems fused into one: a News Feed (Lesson 16) and a media-heavy upload/delivery pipeline (Lesson 12). The staff move is to recognize this immediately, then spend your depth budget on the two load-bearing decisions — how media flows (never through your app servers) and how the feed is assembled (hybrid fan-out). Everything else is plumbing.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “Photos only, or video too?” — Video adds transcoding and adaptive streaming to the media pipeline.
  • “Is the feed chronological or ranked?” — Ranking adds a scoring stage after candidate retrieval.

1 · Functional Requirements

Scope ruthlessly. Three core capabilities, then a couple of supporting ones. Likes, comments, search, Stories, DMs, and ranking are explicitly out of scope [1] — name them so the interviewer knows you saw them, then set them aside.

3 · Capacity Estimation

Only compute the numbers that change a decision. Two matter here: feed read QPS (drives the read architecture) and media storage/bandwidth (drives the blob + CDN decision).

Writes (posts). 100M posts/day ÷ 86,400s ≈ ~1,150 posts/sec average, call it ~3,000/sec at peak. Modest — writes are not the bottleneck.

Reads (feed). 500M DAU each opening the app ~10×/day, each pulling a feed page ≈ 5B feed reads/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ ~58K reads/sec average, ~150–200K/sec peak. Reads dominate by ~50–100× → precompute, cache, never recompute a feed on the hot path.

Media storage. 100M photos/day × ~2 MB original + ~4 derived resolutions (~1 MB total) ≈ ~300 TB/day ≈ ~100 PB/year. Far too large for a database → object storage.

Media bandwidth. Reads × image size dwarfs everything else. Serving this from origin servers is economically impossible → CDN does the heavy lifting; origin (S3) is a cold backstop.

Conclusion in one line: writes are cheap, feed reads are huge but precomputable, and media bytes are the real cost center. That sentence justifies the whole design.

5 · Data Model

Bytes in S3, metadata in a wide-column store, feeds in Redis. Two partition choices carry the design: posts are partitioned by author (profile grid = one partition scan, celebrity pull = one partition read), and the follow graph is stored twice — by follower and by followee — because the read-merge and fan-out paths ask opposite questions.

6 · Data Flow

Instagram has a genuine write-side pipeline, so a flow is worth stating explicitly:

1. Client → POST /v1/media → app server returns presigned URL.
2. Client → uploads bytes straight to S3 (bypassing app servers entirely).
3. S3 event → enqueues a media-processing job (transcode/thumbnail).
4. Worker generates resolutions, writes them back to S3, flips Media → ready.
5. Client → POST /v1/posts → writes Post metadata, enqueues a fan-out job.
6. Fan-out worker pushes the postId into followers' precomputed feed lists.

9 · Deep Dives

Deep dive 1 — Media storage & delivery (never through your app servers)

The single biggest mistake here is routing photo bytes through your application tier. At 300 TB/day of uploads and far more downloads, your app servers would melt and your bandwidth bill would be absurd.

Upload: the client asks for a presigned URL [2] and PUTs bytes directly to S3. Your servers only ever see small JSON. This offloads CPU, memory, and bandwidth, and lets the upload resume/retry against S3's own infrastructure.

Delivery: all media is served through a CDN (CloudFront/Akamai) [2]. The feed returns CDN URLs, not bytes; the client pulls each image from the nearest edge. S3 is the origin of last resort. This is the mechanism that makes "render in < 500 ms globally" achievable.

Multiple resolutions: store the original plus pre-generated thumbnail / feed / full-screen sizes. A phone on cellular gets a 50 KB thumbnail, not a 2 MB original — this is where most of the bandwidth saving actually comes from. See Lesson 45 for the blob + CDN pattern in full.

Deep dive 2 — Feed generation: fan-out on write vs read vs hybrid

This is the heart of the problem and where staff candidates separate themselves. Three strategies, each a different placement of the work.

StrategyHowWinsLoses
Fan-out on read
(pull)		On feed open, query all followees' recent posts and merge.No write amplification; instant for new follows.Expensive read; merges N timelines per request → slow at scale.
Fan-out on write
(push)		On post, write postId into every follower's precomputed feed list.Feed read is one O(1) lookup → fast, cheap reads.Write amplification: a post to 1M followers = 1M writes.
Hybrid ★Push for normal users; pull for celebrities.Cheap reads for the common case; bounds the celebrity blast radius.Read must merge two sources; more moving parts.

Default to fan-out on write: it matches our 100:1 read-heavy profile — pay once at write time so the billions of reads are trivial. A typical post fans out to a few hundred followers, which is cheap.

The killer is the celebrity problem: a user with 100M followers would trigger 100M writes per post and create massive hot-key pressure. The fix is the hybrid model [2]: above a follower threshold, do not fan out. Instead, at feed-read time, pull the celebrity's recent posts on demand and merge them into the precomputed feed. Now a feed read = (your precomputed list from normal followees) ∪ (live fetch of the handful of celebrities you follow). You get fast reads for 99.9% of content and a bounded, cacheable extra query for the few mega-accounts. Lesson 16 introduces fan-out; this is the staff-depth version with the celebrity carve-out made explicit.

★ Staff-level signal The staff candidate names the threshold and what it protects: "I push for accounts under ~10K followers and pull for the rest, because the cost is write-amplification vs. read-merge, and the crossover is where a single post's fan-out would dominate write capacity." Don't just say "hybrid" — state the knob, the failure it prevents (hot keys, write storms), and that the threshold is tunable.

Deep dive 3 — The follow graph & storing posts

The follow graph is an adjacency problem with extreme skew: most users have hundreds of edges, a few have hundreds of millions. Store edges in a sharded store keyed by followerId (for "who do I follow" on the read-merge path) with a secondary index by followeeId (for "who follows me" on the fan-out path). Cache hot edge lists. Posts themselves live in the metadata DB sharded by authorId, so a profile grid is a single-partition range scan — this is why GET /v1/users/{id}/posts is cheap and can skip the feed cache entirely.

Deep dive 4 — Async thumbnail / transcoding via a queue

Generating four resolutions of an 8 MB photo (or transcoding a 4 GB video [1]) is slow and bursty — never do it inline in the request. When the upload lands in S3, an event drops a job onto a message queue, and a pool of workers processes it asynchronously, writing derived assets back to S3 and flipping the Media row to ready. This decouples a slow, CPU-heavy step from the user-facing latency, lets you scale workers independently, and gives you natural retry/back-pressure on failure. See Lesson 07 for the queue-as-buffer pattern. The post can be created immediately and simply show a placeholder until the media is ready — eventual consistency in action.

15 · Privacy

PII: photos (faces, children, locations), captions, the follow graph. EXIF stripping: remove GPS and device metadata from every rendition and the stored original served to others. Private accounts: media URLs signed and short-lived so a leaked link expires. Deletion: deleting a post removes metadata and all S3 renditions, purges the CDN, and feed entries are filtered at hydrate time; account deletion runs a tracked workflow across posts, media, graph edges and backups within the regulatory window. Minors and sensitive content: default-private for teen accounts. Face data: no biometric processing without explicit consent (and not in scope here).

Wrap-up · 58–60 minClose strongRecap decisions and tradeoffs in 60 seconds; name what you would do next.

18 · Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Instagram separates the media path from the metadata path. Clients upload photos directly to S3 with presigned URLs; a queue-driven pipeline creates renditions, and a CDN serves them, so app servers only handle JSON. Feeds are hybrid: post ids are pushed into Redis feeds for normal authors and celebrity posts are merged at read time, keeping reads under 200 ms. Posts and the follow graph live in a partitioned store. At 10× we scale S3, CDN, workers and cache shards; CDN egress is the dominant cost.
