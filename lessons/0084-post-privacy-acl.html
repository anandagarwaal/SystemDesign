Design Post Privacy / Access Control (Facebook/Instagram)

A user picks who can see a post — Only me, Friends, Public, Friends-of-friends, a custom list — and the read path must enforce that choice perfectly, billions of times a day, with zero leaks.

Why this lesson, for your mission: The feed (Lesson 16) is about throughput. Privacy is about correctness under skew — the one design where a single wrong row is a headline, not a latency blip. A senior bolts a visibility column onto posts and filters in a WHERE clause. A staff candidate treats this as an authorization system: deny-by-default, enforced server-side on every read, with the friends-of-friends graph traversal and the "a post's audience changed after fan-out" problem named as the real bottlenecks. This lesson teaches the second posture.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “Which audience types — only me, friends, friends-of-friends, public, custom lists?” — Friends-of-friends is the expensive graph case.
  • “How fast must an unfriend or audience change take effect?” — It bounds the staleness window for cached permissions.
  • “Which surfaces must enforce privacy — feed, search, notifications, APIs?” — Every surface needs the same central check.

Functional Requirements

Privacy is a feature that lives on top of posting and the feed, so scope to the access-control surface only. Four capabilities cover the core.

#Users should be able to…
1Set a post's audience at create/edit time: ONLY_ME, FRIENDS, FRIENDS_OF_FRIENDS, PUBLIC, or a CUSTOM_LIST.
2Have that audience enforced on every read — feed, profile, permalink, search, notifications. A viewer outside the audience must never see the post.
3Manage custom lists (e.g. "Close Friends", "Work") and target a post to one, plus blocklist exclusions.
4Be reachable via friends-of-friends — a viewer who shares at least one mutual friend with the author can see FRIENDS_OF_FRIENDS posts.

Out of scope (state it): ranking, comments/likes privacy, story expiry, GDPR deletion. Reuse the feed and graph from Lessons 16 & 27 — we're adding the authorization layer, not rebuilding them.

Non-Functional Requirements

Here the usual ordering inverts. In the feed lesson we sacrificed consistency for availability. In privacy, correctness is non-negotiable and ranks above availability — it is better to fail closed (show nothing) than to leak one private post.[2]

PropertyTarget / priority
Correctness / no leaksHighest priority — security critical. Zero false-allows. Deny-by-default; fail closed on any error or timeout in the authz path.[2]
Read latencyAuthz check adds < ~10ms p99 to a feed read that already targets <200ms. It runs on every item, so it must be a cache hit, not a graph crawl.
Scale~2B users, ~100B+ relationship edges, millions of feed reads/sec — each fanning into many per-post checks.
Consistency of permissionsAn audience change or unfriend should take effect quickly (seconds) — stale allow is a leak; stale deny is merely annoying. Bound the staleness window explicitly.[1]

Say to move on → “Zero false allows, with correctness ranked above availability. Let me size the checks.”

Phase 2 · 5–10 minEstimate what mattersOnly the numbers that change a decision — say the implication out loud.

Capacity Estimation

Only the numbers that bend the design. Two do: how many authz checks per feed read, and whether friends-of-friends can be computed live.

How many authz checks per second?

  • Say 500M daily feed-openers, ~10 opens/day, ~50 items hydrated per open. That's 500M × 10 × 50 ≈ 250B item-checks/day ≈ ~3M checks/sec average, multiples higher at peak. Implication: the check must be O(1) cache lookups, never a per-item DB or graph query. This is why we precompute, not crawl.

Can friends-of-friends be computed at read time?

  • A user with ~500 friends who each have ~500 friends → a 2-hop neighborhood of up to ~250,000 users. Doing this set-membership test live, for every FRIENDS_OF_FRIENDS item, for millions of readers, is a graph-traversal firehose.[3] Implication: FoF is the expensive case — it forces the denormalize-vs-compute decision (Deep Dive 2).

Interviewer pushback

“Why not traverse the friend graph for each friends-of-friends post?”

Staff answer: A 2-hop neighborhood can be ~250k users and we check ~3M items/s. Cache friend sets and turn it into an in-memory set intersection.

Say to move on → “~3M checks/s must be cache lookups, and friends-of-friends can't be a live graph crawl. Let me define entities.”

Every read surface sends candidate post ids to one authorization service. It evaluates each post's current audience against cached relationships and the graph, and drops anything it can't prove allowed. Only allowed posts are hydrated. Graph and audience changes publish events that invalidate cached relationships within seconds.

High-Level Design

Keep the boxes boring; the judgement is in where the check sits and what it reads. The key move: a dedicated Authorization Service sits on the read path, between the Feed Service and the data, so no read service ever reimplements the policy.

ComponentResponsibility & store
Post ServicePersists posts with their audience field to the Post Store (DynamoDB/Cassandra, keyed by postId).

Read path: GET /feed → Feed Service gathers candidate postIds → for each, Authorization Service checks (viewer, audience) against the graph/cache → only allowed posts are hydrated and returned. Deny-by-default: if the check errors or times out, the item is dropped, never shown.[2]

“Why a dedicated authorization service instead of WHERE clauses in each service?”

Staff answer: Five read surfaces with hand-written filters means five places to leak. One service gives one policy and one audit surface.

Phase 5 · 27–47 minDeep dives & tradeoffsGo deep where the system breaks; compare alternatives and justify the pick.

Deep Dive 1 — The check on the read path: deny-by-default, never trust the client

The single most important sentence in this interview: authorization is enforced on the read path, server-side, for every item, and it fails closed. Privacy that lives only at write time (e.g. "we don't fan out private posts to the wrong feeds") is not enforcement — it's an optimization that breaks the moment anything reaches a feed it shouldn't, or a permalink is guessed.

Where to enforce

Centralize the check in one Authorization Service that every read path calls. If the Feed Service, the profile page, search, and notifications each hand-roll a WHERE visibility = ... clause, you have five places to leak from and five places to keep in sync. One service = one policy = one audit surface.[1]

Deny-by-default, concretely (per secure-coding rules):

  • Default deny: the check returns deny unless an explicit rule grants access. New audience types are invisible until a rule is written — fail safe, not fail open.
  • Server-side only: the viewer identity comes from the auth token; the audience is read from trusted storage. Never accept a client-supplied "canView" flag or trust client-side filtering.[2]
  • Fail closed: if the graph lookup times out or the cache is down, drop the item. A slow feed is acceptable; a leaked post is not.
  • 404 over 403: on a permalink, deny by returning "not found" so you don't confirm a private post exists.

Deep Dive 2 — Modeling visibility + the graph: why friends-of-friends is expensive

Two pieces: the audience field on the post (the policy) and the social graph the policy is evaluated against (the data). The cheap audiences are O(1): PUBLIC always allows, ONLY_ME allows only the author, CUSTOM_LIST is a set-membership test, and FRIENDS is a single "are viewer and author friends?" edge lookup.

Friends-of-friends is the hard one because it is a 2-hop graph reachability question: does the viewer share any mutual friend with the author? Answering live means intersecting two friend-sets of up to ~500 each, or crawling a neighborhood of up to ~250k users — per item, per reader.[3] At ~3M checks/sec, live traversal is a non-starter.

AudienceCheckCost
PUBLIC / ONLY_MEConstant ruleO(1)
FRIENDSOne edge lookupO(1) point read
CUSTOM_LISTSet membershipO(1) hashed
FRIENDS_OF_FRIENDS2-hop intersectionExpensive — graph traversal

Denormalize vs. compute

The classic staff tradeoff. Compute on read keeps storage minimal and is always fresh, but pays the traversal cost on the hot path — fatal at this QPS. Denormalize (precompute and store the answer) makes the read O(1) but costs storage and, crucially, must be invalidated when the graph changes.

DimensionCompute on read (traverse)Denormalize (precompute)
Read latencySlow — 2-hop crawlFast — point lookup
FreshnessAlways currentNeeds invalidation
StorageMinimalHigher (mutual-friend cache)
Best forRare / admin checksHot-path FoF at scale

The pragmatic pick: don't materialize the full FoF set. Instead, on a feed read cache the viewer's friend-id set in Redis, and check FoF as a cheap set intersection against the author's friends — converting a graph crawl into an in-memory hash intersection, refreshed on a short TTL.

Deep Dive 3 — Privacy × fan-out: when a post's audience changes

This is the deep dive that ties back to Lesson 16. If feeds are precomputed (fan-out on write), a post's postId is already sitting in thousands of materialized feeds. Now the author changes the audience from PUBLIC to FRIENDS, or unfriends someone. The fanned-out entries are now stale and potentially leaking.

StrategyHow privacy stays correctCost
Re-check on read (chosen)Feed stores only postIds; the authz check runs at hydration, reading the post's current audience.One check per item (cached)
Scrub fanned-out feedsOn audience change, find and remove the post from every feed it was pushed to.Huge write amplification
Trust write-time placementAssume "it was correct when fanned out."Leaks — never do this

The resolution: the precomputed feed is a list of candidates, not a list of permissions. Fan-out decides what might be relevant; the read-path authz check decides what's allowed right now. This is exactly why Deep Dive 1's "enforce on read" rule is non-negotiable — it's what makes a stale fanned-out entry harmless. You never need to scrub feeds; the check at read time always reflects the latest audience and graph.

★ Staff-level signal The senior says "filter by visibility." The staff candidate sees the interaction between two subsystems: precomputed fan-out makes feeds fast, but it also means permission state is duplicated and goes stale. They resolve it cleanly — "feeds cache candidates, not authority; the read-path check is the source of truth, so audience changes and unfriends are honored without ever rewriting a single fanned-out feed." Naming that separation (generation vs. authorization, just as the feed lesson separated generation vs. ranking) and bounding the permission-staleness window is the move that says staff.

Deep Dive 4 — ReBAC / Zanzibar-style checks & caching authz decisions safely

Relationship-based access control (ReBAC)

Post privacy is fundamentally a relationship question — "can this viewer see this post?" depends on the friend edge between them, not on a static role. That's ReBAC, and the canonical implementation is Google's Zanzibar, which stores relationships as tuples like post:123#viewer@user:42 and answers "check" queries by walking those relations.[1] Mentioning Zanzibar (and that it backs a dedicated authorization service — Lesson 68) signals you know this is a solved, productized problem, not something to hand-roll per feature.

Caching authz decisions without leaking

To hit <10ms we must cache, but a stale allow is a leak. The safe pattern, modeled on Zanzibar's consistency tokens:[1]

  • Cache the relationship, not the verdict, when possible: caching "viewer⇄author are friends" is reusable across all their posts; invalidate it on unfriend.
  • Short TTL + explicit invalidation: bound staleness (e.g. seconds), and proactively bust the cache on unfriend, block, audience change, or list edit.
  • Bias toward stale-deny, never stale-allow: if unsure whether a cached allow is current, re-check. Erring closed keeps a leak from ever happening.[2]
  • Consistency tokens: stamp each decision with the graph version it was computed against, so you can require "at least as fresh as the unfriend event" for sensitive checks.[1]

Privacy

The whole feature is privacy, so the extra obligations are about side channels. Existence leaks: return 404 on deny; comment and like counts, search snippets, notifications and link previews all go through the same check. Graph leaks: friend lists and list membership are themselves private (you can't see who is on someone's Close Friends list). Caches and logs: decision caches hold ids, not content; audit logs are restricted and retained ~90 days. Deletion: deleting a post or account removes tuples and busts caches. Data subject requests: "who could see this?" answered by expanding the audience at the current token.

Interviewer pushback

“How do you cache decisions without leaking?”

Staff answer: Cache relationships rather than verdicts, invalidate on unfriend, block and audience change, keep short TTLs, prefer stale deny over stale allow, and use consistency tokens for sensitive checks.

Wrap-up · 58–60 minClose strongRecap decisions and tradeoffs in 60 seconds; name what you would do next.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Post privacy is an authorization system. Every read surface sends candidate posts to one central ReBAC service that evaluates each post's current audience against the graph: public, friends, friends-of-friends via cached friend-set intersection, custom lists and blocks. It is deny-by-default and fails closed on errors. Feeds only cache candidates, so audience changes and unfriends take effect at read time without rewriting feeds. Relationship caches are invalidated by events, and shadow evaluation and leak canaries prove zero false allows. At 10× we add cache shards.

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. Why must the audience check run on the read path, not only at write time?

A precomputed feed holds candidate postIds; an unfriend or audience change makes those stale, so only the read-time check reflects current truth.

2. Why is friends-of-friends the expensive audience type to enforce?

FoF asks "do viewer and author share a mutual friend" — a 2-hop intersection over up to ~250k users, far costlier than an O(1) edge lookup.

3. What does "deny-by-default, fail closed" mean for the authz path here?

Failing closed means a slow or broken check drops the item; a stale allow is a leak, so the safe default is always to deny.

4. What is the safe way to cache authorization decisions for speed?

Caching the reusable relationship (not the per-post verdict) with bounded TTL plus explicit invalidation on unfriend/audience change avoids stale-allow leaks.
