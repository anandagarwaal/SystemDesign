Design Flight Search / Aggregator (Skyscanner / Google Flights)

You don't own the inventory. You fan out to dozens of slow, flaky airline and GDS providers and still have to answer in under a second.

Why this lesson, for your mission: A flight aggregator is the canonical scatter-gather over untrusted upstreams problem. The L6/L7 signal isn't drawing a search index — it's naming up front that you control none of the data sources, that they're slow and unreliable, and that the entire design is a negotiation between freshness (real prices that won't get rejected at booking), latency (sub-second results), and cost (every upstream call is money). Get the timeout/partial-results posture and the cache-TTL tradeoff right and you've shown the judgement that separates senior from staff.

Functional Requirements

Three capabilities define the product. Everything else is deferred out loud.

#A user should be able to…
1Search flights by origin, destination, and date(s) — one-way, round-trip, and multi-leg.
2Filter and sort results by price, airline, stops, and total duration.
3See fresh, bookable prices and then redirect to the airline/OTA to complete the booking.

Out of scope (say it): we are a metasearch engine — we do not hold inventory, take payment, issue tickets, or own the booking transaction. We aggregate and route the user out. That single sentence reframes the whole problem away from a transactional booking system.[1]

Capacity Estimation

Only the numbers that change a decision. These are my own back-of-envelope estimates.

  • Searches: ~100M searches/day ≈ ~1,200 QPS average, ~5–10k QPS at peak. This is the read load the cache+aggregation tier must absorb.
  • Upstream fan-out: each search hits ~30–50 providers. A naive cache miss = ~50 outbound calls × 1,200 QPS = ~60k upstream calls/sec — economically impossible. The cache hit rate is the whole design.
  • Route × date space: ~10k airports → millions of O&D pairs × ~330 future dates. Far too large to pre-compute fully → cache popular routes, query the long tail live.
  • Fare cache size: say 1M hot route-date keys × a few KB of priced itineraries ≈ low tens of GB — fits comfortably in a Redis tier; this is not a storage problem, it's a freshness problem.
Takeaways: the two scaling problems are the fan-out (latency + cost of slow upstreams) and the cache (hit rate vs staleness). User reads are otherwise cheap.

Data Flow

This is an aggregation system, so trace one search end to end before drawing boxes:

  1. Search request — API normalizes the query (canonical O&D + date + pax) into a cache key.
  2. Cache lookup — return any fresh cached fares for that key immediately as the first partial.
  3. Scatter — for misses / stale keys, fan out async requests to all relevant providers in parallel.
  4. Gather with timeout — collect responses until a hard deadline (~1–2s); whatever arrives is used.
  5. Normalize & merge — map each provider's schema to your itinerary model, dedupe, rank, cache with TTL.
  6. Serve + refresh — return ranked results; async pipeline keeps popular routes warm in the background.

Step 3–4 are the scatter-gather problem; step 2/5 are the caching/freshness problem; step 6 is the async refresh problem; building multi-leg itineraries from segments is the graph problem. Each is a deep dive.

Deep Dive 1 — Scatter-gather over slow, unreliable upstreams

You query 30–50 providers; some answer in 80ms, some in 4s, some time out, one is down. The naive design awaits all of them and is held hostage by the slowest (or the dead) one. The pattern is scatter-gather with a hard deadline and partial results.[1]

Fan out in parallel, gather until a deadline, return what you have
  • Per-provider timeout (e.g. 1.5s) and an overall request deadline. When the deadline hits, you ship whatever returned — a search with 45/50 providers is still a great search.
  • Circuit breakers per provider: after N consecutive failures, trip open and stop calling it for a cooldown. A dead upstream costs you nothing instead of eating a timeout every search.
  • Bulkheads: isolate each provider's thread/connection pool so one slow upstream can't exhaust resources and stall calls to the healthy ones.
  • Hedging & retries only where safe (idempotent reads); back off and never retry a tripped provider.

Combine this with the cache: the cache answers instantly as the first partial, then live provider responses patch in fresher prices as they arrive — so the user sees something in <100ms and the page sharpens over the next second.

★ Staff-level signal Stating "I don't control these upstreams, so I design for partial results under a deadline, not for completeness" within the first two minutes is the whole interview. Adding circuit breakers + bulkheads shows you treat a failing dependency as the expected case. The senior answer waits for everyone; the staff answer decides what "good enough, on time" means and engineers the timeout budget to hit it.

Deep Dive 2 — Caching fares: TTL and the freshness-vs-cost tradeoff

Live-querying every provider per search would be both too slow and ruinously expensive (~60k upstream calls/sec at peak). So you cache priced itineraries. But fares are volatile — seats sell, prices move minute-to-minute — and a stale fare that gets rejected at the airline is the worst user experience. This is a pure freshness-vs-cost dial.[2]

LeverWhat it buys / costs
Route-aware TTLHot routes (busy O&D, near-term dates) get short TTLs (~minutes); cold long-tail routes get longer TTLs. Spends freshness where it matters.[2]
Stale-while-revalidateServe the cached fare instantly, kick an async refresh; next searcher gets the fresh one. Hides latency without paying it inline.
Re-validate on intentOn redirect, re-price that one itinerary live before handing off — cheap (one call) and kills most "price changed" rejections.
Mark staleness in UI"Price last checked 3 min ago" sets expectations and lets you run longer TTLs safely.
★ Staff-level signal The naive answer is "cache fares for 5 minutes." The staff answer is "TTL is not a constant — it's a function of route volatility and search volume, and I re-validate the single chosen fare at redirect time so I get cheap caching everywhere and a correct price at the moment of truth." Naming the failure mode (a rejected fare destroys trust) and dialing TTL to it is the judgement being tested.

Deep Dive 3 — Multi-leg itinerary search (the flight graph)

Most cheap itineraries aren't single non-stops — they're stitched from segments across airlines ("self-transfer"), e.g. AMS→DXB→SIN. Model flights as a graph: nodes are airport-time states, edges are segments, and a valid itinerary is a path that respects connection constraints.

Constrained graph search, not naive shortest path
  • Time-expanded graph: a node is (airport, time); an edge connects an arrival to a later departure only if the layover is ≥ minimum connection time and within a max.
  • Bound the explosion: cap stops (usually ≤2), cap total duration, prune by a max layover window. Combinatorics explode fast — pruning aggressively is mandatory.
  • Multi-objective: users sort by price or duration or stops — so you keep a Pareto frontier of itineraries, not a single "best" path.
  • Pre-compute schedules, price late: build candidate itineraries from the (slow-changing) schedule graph, then price only the survivors via the fare cache / providers.

The key separation again: schedules are stable and live in an indexable graph you can search cheaply; fares are volatile and fetched only for the handful of candidate itineraries you actually surface. Searching the graph on priced data per request would be both slow and wasteful.

Deep Dive 4 — Async price-refresh pipeline

To keep cache hit rates high and fresh, you don't wait for user searches to populate the cache — you proactively refresh popular keys in the background, just like the price-tracker's polling pipeline.[1]

Scheduler → Queue → Refresh workers
  • A scheduler enqueues "re-price route R on date D" jobs, prioritized by search popularity and volatility — busy near-term routes refresh every few minutes; the long tail rarely or never.[2]
  • A queue decouples scheduling from execution, absorbs bursts, and provides backpressure when providers are slow or rate-limited.
  • Workers call provider adapters (respecting per-provider rate limits + circuit breakers), normalize, and write fresh fares into the cache with a TTL.
  • Failed refreshes back off and dead-letter; a route whose refresh fails simply falls back to live fan-out on the next user search.

This turns most user searches into pure cache hits answered in milliseconds, while the expensive upstream traffic happens off the critical path — the same "move work out of the request path" judgement L6/L7 rewards across every read-heavy system.

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. Providers are slow and some are down. How should a search behave?

Scatter-gather with a deadline plus circuit breakers gives sub-second, resilient searches.

2. Why is the fare cache hit rate the heart of the design?

Misses multiply into tens of thousands of upstream calls/sec — hit rate controls latency and cost.

3. How do you keep cheap caching yet a correct price at booking time?

Route-aware TTL caches everywhere; a single live re-price at redirect kills most rejections.

4. Why separate flight schedules from fares into different stores?

Search the stable schedule graph cheaply; fetch volatile fares only for surviving candidates.
