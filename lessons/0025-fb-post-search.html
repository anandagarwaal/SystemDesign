Design Facebook Post Search

Search a corpus of billions of posts in under half a second — and keep it fresh as new posts arrive.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Search problems separate the candidates who reach for "use Elasticsearch" from those who can explain what Elasticsearch is doing. At L6/L7 the interviewer is probing whether you understand the inverted index, the document-vs-term sharding tradeoff, and the scatter-gather + two-phase ranking pattern that every large-scale search system is built on. Naming a tool is senior; deriving the mechanics is staff.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “Is it keyword match only, or also fuzzy and personalized ranking?” — Exact keyword with recency/likes sort keeps the index simple.
  • “How fresh must new posts be in search?” — A sub-minute target allows a near-real-time indexing pipeline.
  • “Do likes need to affect ranking instantly?” — Approximate like counts let us batch index updates.

Functional Requirements

Search is deceptively simple to state. Lock the scope before designing.

#The user should be able to…
1Create posts and like posts (this is the write/index volume that shapes everything).[1]
2Search posts by keyword.[1]
3Sort results by recency or by like count (relevance proxy).[1]

Explicitly out of scope: fuzzy matching, personalization, privacy/visibility rules, media, and full ML ranking.[1] Saying these out loud signals you know where the rabbit holes are and are choosing not to fall in.

The two tensions to name up front.
  • Freshness vs. cost — sub-minute indexing means a streaming pipeline, not nightly batch.
  • Latency vs. corpus size — you cannot scan petabytes per query, so the entire design hinges on the inverted index.

Staff answer: 100k like writes/s would thrash index segments. Batch likes and update the stored count only at milestones (10, 100, 1000…), since the rank order barely changes between them.

Core Entities & API

Entities: User (creates posts), Post (text + timestamp + likeCount), Like (aggregated into a count, not stored per-row in the index).[1]

Note cursor, not page: offset pagination breaks when the index is changing under you. Cursor pagination (encode "last post ID / score seen") is the staff-correct choice for a live corpus.

Data Model

The source of truth (posts, like counts) lives in the post and like services; the search index is a derived, rebuildable projection fed from Kafka. Index shards are partitioned by document and tiered by time — recent posts on hot SSD nodes, older posts in warm/cold tiers — because most queries want recent or popular results.

Why does the search API use a cursor rather than a page number?

A live, constantly-updated index shifts offsets underneath a paging client; a cursor tied to a stable score/id survives that.

Why is the search index treated as a "derived, rebuildable projection" rather than a source of truth?

4 of 7 — High-level design

Phase 4 · 17–27 minHigh-level designA simple end-to-end design that satisfies every functional requirement.

Data Flow — post → searchable

Because this is a data-processing pipeline, trace one post from creation to being searchable. This is the ingestion path and it's where freshness is won or lost.

  1. Write: Post Service persists the post and emits an event to Kafka.[1]
  2. Ingest: A pool of Indexer consumers reads the partition.
  3. Analyze: Each indexer tokenizes the text — lowercase, split on whitespace/punctuation, drop stop-words, optionally stem ("running" → "run").
  4. Update index: For each resulting term, append the post ID to that term's posting list (and to the per-term sorted structure used for ranking).[1]
  5. Searchable: Once the posting lists are updated, the post surfaces in queries — target < 1 min end-to-end.

Kafka decouples the write spike from the indexers, gives replay/durability, and lets you add consumers to scale ingestion independently of the post API.

5 of 7 — Deep dives & tradeoffs

Phase 5 · 27–47 minDeep dives & tradeoffsGo deep where the system breaks; compare alternatives and justify the pick.

Deep Dive 1 — The inverted index (and how to shard it)

Don't say "use Elasticsearch" and move on. Elasticsearch is a distributed inverted index built on Lucene — explain the mechanics it hides.

What the index actually is

A forward index maps document → terms. Searching it means scanning every document — impossible at 3.6 trillion posts. An inverted index flips it: term → posting list, where the posting list is the set of post IDs containing that term (often with positions and a per-document weight).

"taylor"  → [p17, p93, p412, p9981, ...]
"swift"   → [p17, p93, p55, p9981, ...]
"concert" → [p412, p9981, ...]

A keyword search is now a lookup, not a scan. A multi-word query is an intersection of posting lists.

Sharding: by term vs. by document

The corpus doesn't fit on one node, so the index must be partitioned. There are exactly two ways, and naming the tradeoff is the staff signal.

Term-partitioned (by keyword)Document-partitioned (by post ID)
LayoutEach shard owns a slice of terms; that term's entire posting list lives there.Each shard owns a slice of posts and a full mini-index over them.
Query workRoute to the few shards holding the query's terms — low fan-out.Every shard must be queried (scatter-gather) — high fan-out.
Hot-spot riskHigh — "the" or a viral term lands one giant list on one node.Low — load spreads evenly across shards.
IndexingOne post touches many term-shards (one write per term).One post → one shard. Simple, local.
The pick: document-partitioned, which is what Elasticsearch does by default. You pay fan-out (every shard queried per search) to win even load distribution, simple local indexing, and no catastrophic term hot-spots. The fan-out cost is exactly what Deep Dive 4 solves with scatter-gather.

Deep Dive 2 — Ranking: recency vs. relevance, at scale

Returning matches is easy; returning the right matches in 500ms is the hard part.

Two sort orders, two structures

Rather than sort millions of matches at query time, maintain per-term ordered structures so the top results fall out cheaply.[1]

  • Recency: append-only posting list, read from the tail — newest first, no sorting needed.[1]
  • Likes: a sorted set keyed by like count, so the top-N by engagement is a range read.[1]

This doubles index storage but eliminates request-time sorting — a classic write-cost-for-read-speed trade.[1]

The two-phase pattern (retrieve, then rank)

The pattern behind every large search engine: don't score the whole posting list, score a candidate set.

  1. Retrieve (cheap): pull the top N×2 candidates from the index using the coarse stored order.
  2. Rank (expensive): re-score just those candidates with fresher / richer signals — e.g. live like counts from the Like Service, or eventually an ML model — then return the top N.[1]
★ Staff-level signal Two-phase ranking is the lever that lets the index store only an approximate order (cheap to maintain) while still serving accurate top results (re-ranked on a tiny candidate set). It's why you can be loose about exact like counts in the index — you fix it at rank time over N×2 rows, not over the whole list.

Deep Dive 3 — Keeping the index fresh (NRT pipeline)

The < 1-minute freshness NFR forces a near-real-time pipeline, but the 100k likes/s NFR forbids the obvious implementation. Resolve the tension.

The pipeline

Posts flow Post Service → Kafka → Indexers. Kafka partitions absorb the 10k posts/s spike; indexers consume and update posting lists.[1] Add indexers to raise throughput; replay a partition to rebuild a corrupt shard.

The like problem — batch and milestone

Re-indexing on every like means 100k index writes/s, and a viral post alone can fire thousands. Two moves:[1]

  • Window batching: aggregate likes over a ~30s window — a viral post's hundreds of likes collapse into one incremented write.[1]
  • Milestone updates: only re-rank at thresholds (powers of 2 or 10), turning 1,000 likes into ~10 index writes; exact counts are recovered at rank time.[1]
Indexing latency vs. cost. Tighter windows / lower thresholds = fresher like counts but far more index writes and CPU. Because two-phase ranking re-scores candidates with live counts anyway, the index can afford a coarse, cheap approximation. Freshness for new posts stays sub-minute; freshness for like counts is deliberately relaxed.

Deep Dive 4 — Scaling reads: scatter-gather & caching

With a document-partitioned index, every query fans out to all shards. Make that fast and cheap.

Scatter-gather

  1. Scatter: Search Service sends the query to every index shard in parallel.
  2. Local top-K: each shard returns its own top K for the sort order — small payloads.
  3. Gather + merge: coordinator merges the per-shard top-K into a global top-K (a k-way merge), then applies phase-two re-ranking.

Latency is bounded by the slowest shard, so this is also a tail-latency problem: hedge requests, or return after the fastest S of S+r replicas respond.

Caching hot queries

Search traffic is Zipfian — a few queries dominate. A distributed cache with a short TTL (matching the freshness budget, ~1 min) deduplicates repeated searches; a CDN/edge layer with cache-control headers can drop popular-query latency to tens of milliseconds.[1]

Storage relief (bonus). Cap posting lists to 1k–10k entries per term, and move rarely-queried terms' lists to blob storage (S3/R2) via batch jobs — check Redis first, fall back to blob on a miss with an acceptable latency penalty.[1] Most searches want recent/popular posts, so the cap rarely bites.

Staff answer: By document: shard load stays even and a post is written to one shard. Term sharding creates hot shards for popular words and multi-shard writes per post. We pay for it with scatter-gather on queries.

Check before continuing

What do you trade away by choosing document-partitioned sharding over term-partitioned?

Doc-partitioning avoids hot term shards and keeps indexing simple, but every query must be scattered to all shards since any shard might hold a match.

Reread "The pick" box in Deep Dive 1.

Why does two-phase ranking let the index tolerate an approximate, milestone-based like count instead of an exact one?

The index only needs to produce a reasonable candidate set cheaply; correctness of the final ranking is restored by the second, expensive pass over just those candidates.

Where does the "fresh" like count actually get applied — retrieval or ranking?

Why does term-partitioned sharding create hot shards that document-partitioned sharding avoids?

Since one shard owns a whole term's posting list, a viral or common term ("the," a trending topic) sends all of its traffic and storage to that single shard. Doc-partitioning spreads any one term's postings across many shards instead.

If shard placement is by term, where does all the traffic for one especially popular term go?

Privacy

PII: post text, authorship, and — most sensitive — search queries, which reveal intent. Deletion is the hard part for search: a deleted or privacy-changed post must disappear from results fast; deletes flow through the same Kafka topic as tombstones, and the search service post-filters against the post store so a not-yet-reindexed deleted post is never returned. Query logs: kept with hashed user ids, aggregated after 30 days, used for query suggestions only in k-anonymous form. Cold tiers and snapshots must honor deletion too — apply tombstones on segment rewrite and expire backups. Account deletion: delete-by-author across indices via author_id.

Wrap-up · 58–60 minClose strongRecap decisions and tradeoffs in 60 seconds; name what you would do next.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Post search is an inverted index fed by a write-heavy pipeline. Posts and likes flow through Kafka to indexers that update document-sharded indexes within a minute, with likes batched and applied at milestones. Queries scatter to shards, retrieve top candidates by recency or approximate likes, re-rank a small set with exact counts, and filter results through privacy checks. Recent data lives on a hot tier and older data on cheaper warm tiers. At 10× we add partitions, indexers and replicas without changing the design.
