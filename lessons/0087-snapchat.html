Design Snapchat (Ephemeral Messaging)

Snapchat looks like a media-messaging app, but the interview-defining twist is the inverse of every other system: the hard requirement is not to keep data — it is to delete it, reliably, on a deadline.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Snapchat is the canonical "ephemerality" interview. At L6/L7 the bar is not "draw a chat app with photos" — it is to recognize that deletion is a first-class feature with its own correctness guarantee, then design a media lifecycle (upload → CDN deliver → TTL delete) and be honest that "true" deletion is impossible across caches, CDNs, and client devices. Naming what you can guarantee versus what you can only discourage is the staff move.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “What does 'deleted' mean — from servers, CDN, devices?” — We can guarantee server-side deletion, but only discourage copies on devices.
  • “How long do unopened snaps and stories live?” — TTL windows bound resident storage.
  • “Is end-to-end encryption required?” — E2EE changes deletion to key destruction and removes server scanning.

Functional Requirements

Scope tightly. Snap messaging + stories, with auto-deletion as the load-bearing capability. Defer the rest out loud.

#Users should be able to…
1Send a disappearing photo/video snap to one or more friends.
2View a snap once, after which it auto-deletes (and expires unopened after a window).
3Post a story visible to friends that auto-expires 24 hours after posting.
4Capture and attach media (image/video) that streams back fast on view.

Out of scope (say so out loud): the camera/AR lens pipeline, Snap Map, Discover content, group video calls, and end-to-end encryption (acknowledged, not designed).

Non-Functional Requirements

The "-ilities" drive every decision below. The unusual one — and the one to lead with — is the ephemerality guarantee.

Phase 2 · 5–10 minEstimate what mattersOnly the numbers that change a decision — say the implication out loud.

Capacity Estimation

Estimate only what changes the design. For Snapchat the load-bearing numbers are media bytes (storage + egress), not metadata rows. The following are my own back-of-envelope estimates.

  • Snaps/day: ~5B snaps/day ≈ ~60K snaps/s average, peaking several × higher in the evening. Each is a small metadata row + one media blob.
  • Media size: a compressed snap averages ~1–5 MB. At 5B/day × ~2 MB that is ~10 PB/day of writes — but most of it is short-lived, so steady-state storage is bounded by the TTL window, not the cumulative total.
  • Storage is a flow, not a stock: because snaps are deleted on view and stories after 24h, retained bytes ≈ (one day of unopened snaps + one day of stories), perhaps low single-digit PB resident — orders of magnitude smaller than a "keep forever" design. This is the design-changing number.
  • Read egress: stories are read by many friends, so view bandwidth > upload bandwidth. That gap is exactly what a CDN exists to absorb.

Implication: the system is bytes-dominated and ephemeral, so the architecture is blob store + CDN + a relentless TTL reaper; the metadata/messaging tier is comparatively cheap.

Interviewer pushback

“If uploads are 10 PB a day, isn't storage enormous?”

Staff answer: Storage is a flow, not a stock: viewed snaps delete in minutes and stories in 24 hours, so resident storage is a few PB. Egress is the real cost.

Say to move on → “~10 PB/day in, but resident storage is bounded by TTLs. Let me define entities, each with an expiry.”

Check before continuing

Why is resident storage only a few PB despite ~10 PB/day of uploads?

Ephemerality bounds resident storage to roughly one day of unopened snaps plus one day of stories, regardless of cumulative upload volume.

Core Entities & API

Entities: User, Snap (metadata: sender, media pointer, expiry, view state), Media (the blob + CDN URL), Recipient (per-friend delivery + view record), and Story (a user's 24h-TTL collection of snaps).

Bytes go directly to blob storage; only small metadata touches the app tier. The user is always derived from the auth token, never passed in the body.

Data Model

Every row and every blob is born with an expiry. Metadata lives in a KV store with native per-item TTL. Media lives in a blob store under a key prefix whose lifecycle rule expires objects past the maximum window. Each recipient has their own row, so view state and deletion are tracked per recipient, and the shared blob is removed only when the last recipient is done.

Control plane: the API mints pre-signed URLs, writes snap and inbox rows (each with a TTL) and notifies recipients through the WhatsApp-style delivery path. Data plane: bytes go sender → blob store → CDN → recipient and never touch app servers. On view or expiry, the reaper deletes the blob and purges the CDN; store-level TTLs are the safety net if it doesn't run. Stories are the same pipeline with a 24-hour expiry, read via fan-out-on-read.

High-Level Design

The defining move is separating the tiny control plane (metadata, messaging, lifecycle decisions) from the fat data plane (media bytes that flow blob → CDN → device, never through app servers).

Phase 5 · 27–47 minDeep dives & tradeoffsGo deep where the system breaks; compare alternatives and justify the pick.

Deep Dives

1. Ephemeral media lifecycle: store → deliver → delete

This is the heart of the problem. A snap's metadata moves through an explicit state machine, and each transition has a deletion consequence:

StateWhat's trueDeletion trigger
UPLOADEDBlob in storage, not yet sent.Orphan sweep if never attached to a snap.
DELIVEREDRecipient notified, not yet opened.Unopened TTL (e.g. 30 days) expires it.
VIEWEDRecipient opened it.Delete immediately after view (per recipient).
EXPIREDAll recipients viewed or TTL hit.Reaper removes blob + metadata + CDN copy.

Two complementary mechanisms do the deleting. Passive TTL: every metadata row and every blob object carries an expiry timestamp, and the store auto-evicts on its own schedule — this is your safety net so nothing lives forever even if the active path fails. Active deletion: on the /view call (or when the last recipient views), a lifecycle worker proactively deletes the blob and purges it from the CDN so it stops being servable in seconds, not days. You want both: active for the promptness users expect, passive TTL as the guarantee that holds even when a worker crashes.

★ Staff-level signal Say this explicitly: "Deletion is a feature with its own SLA, so I make it idempotent and back it with a passive TTL, not just an active delete." A senior wires up a "delete on view" handler and calls it done. A staff engineer asks "what happens if that handler never runs?" and answers it: the row and blob also carry a TTL, so the worst case is delayed deletion, never permanent retention. Designing the fallback path for a correctness-critical action — and making delete safe to run twice — is the difference.

2. The deletion guarantee — and why "true" deletion is hard

Be honest in the interview: you can guarantee deletion from systems you control, but you cannot guarantee a snap is gone from the universe. Walk through where copies leak and what you actually do about each:

Where a copy can surviveWhat you can do
Origin blob store + metadataFully controllable — active delete + TTL guarantees removal.
CDN edge cachesMostly — short cache TTLs + explicit purge API; brief windows remain.
Recipient's device cacheDiscourage — render in-app, don't write to gallery, clear on view.
Screenshots / a second cameraCannot prevent — only detect (screenshot notification) and notify the sender.

So the honest claim is: "We guarantee server-side deletion within seconds of view and at-most-TTL otherwise; CDN copies expire within their cache window; client and out-of-band copies we can only discourage and detect." Stating that boundary precisely — rather than promising magic — is what an L6/L7 interviewer is listening for. The screenshot-detection trick is the classic "you can't stop it, so surface it" judgement call.

3. Media upload to blob + CDN delivery

Never stream large media through your application servers — it wastes CPU, memory, and bandwidth on a tier that should stay thin. The pattern (full treatment in Lesson 45 · Blob Store [2]):

  • Upload: client asks for a pre-signed URL and PUTs bytes directly to the blob store; the app server only ever sees the small mediaId, never the bytes [2].
  • Deliver: on view, the client fetches the media from a CDN URL; the CDN pulls from the origin blob once and serves cached copies near each viewer, giving the <1s open. This matters most for stories, where one upload is read by many friends.
  • Ephemerality wrinkle: because deletion is the goal, keep CDN cache TTLs short and call the CDN's purge/invalidation API on delete, so an expired snap stops being servable quickly. This is the tension unique to Snapchat — CDNs are built to cache aggressively, but you need them to forget aggressively.

This is the same upload/CDN split you'd describe for Instagram (Lesson 27); the only difference is that Instagram caches forever and Snapchat deliberately purges.

4. Messaging / delivery path + read-receipt-triggered deletion

The "get a snap to a friend, online or offline" sub-problem is exactly the WhatsApp problem — don't reinvent it, reuse it (see Lesson 19 · WhatsApp [1]). A persistent connection plus a connection registry routes the "you have a snap" notification to an online recipient; a durable per-recipient inbox holds it for offline users until they reconnect and ACK [1]. The snap metadata rides this path; the bytes always come separately from the CDN.

The Snapchat-specific twist: the read receipt and the deletion trigger are the same event. When the recipient opens the snap and the client posts /view, that single signal (a) tells the sender "opened" (read receipt) and (b) marks the recipient row VIEWED, which enqueues deletion once all recipients have viewed. One action does double duty — receipt and reaper share a trigger. For a snap sent to several friends, you delete per recipient and only purge the shared blob once the last recipient has viewed or all their TTLs lapse.

5. Stories fan-out with a 24h TTL

A story is a one-to-many read problem with a hard expiry, which makes it a clean fan-out-on-read case. Contrast the two approaches:

ApproachVerdict
Fan-out-on-write: copy each story entry into every friend's feedWasteful — duplicates large media pointers across all friends, and every copy needs its own 24h TTL cleanup.
Fan-out-on-read: store the story once, friends pull live entries on openBetter — one copy, one TTL; friends query "your friends' non-expired stories" at read time.

Because stories are pulled when a user opens the app (not pushed instantly like a snap), read-time fan-in is fine and avoids write amplification. Expiry is trivial with a per-entry TTL: each story snap row carries createdAt + 24h; the store auto-evicts it and the reaper purges the underlying blob + CDN copy. The read query simply filters expiry > now, so even before physical deletion runs, an expired entry is never shown. That "filter on read, delete in background" pattern means correctness (what users see) never waits on the reaper.

Privacy

Ephemerality is the privacy promise, so state it precisely. Server-side: viewed snaps are deleted within minutes, unopened ones within 30 days, and stories at 24 hours. Backups exclude media, and metadata backups age out within days. Honest limits: screenshots and second cameras can't be prevented, only detected and notified. Minimization: view receipts and story viewer lists expire with the content, and analytics use counts, not content. Legal holds and safety: reported content is preserved under restricted access for trust & safety or law enforcement. Tell users this in the policy so "ephemeral" isn't misleading. Minors: stricter defaults (friends-only messaging, no stranger discovery).

10× = 50B snaps/day (~600k/s average).

First bottleneck: the CDN purge API rate limits and blob delete throughput. Use batch and tag-based purges, and rely on short cache TTLs rather than a purge per object. What forces a redesign: a requirement for cryptographically guaranteed deletion everywhere, which moves to end-to-end encryption (approach B) and gives up server-side scanning.

Cost of Running

Assumptions: 5B snaps/day at ~2 MB (~10 PB/day uploaded), ~1.2 views per snap + stories, so ~12 PB/day of egress; ~3 PB resident; ~15B metadata writes/day. Wrap-up · 58–60 minClose strongRecap decisions and tradeoffs in 60 seconds; name what you would do next.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Snapchat treats deletion as a feature with an SLA. The control plane writes snap, inbox and story rows that each carry an expiry, and notifies recipients through a messaging delivery path. The data plane sends bytes sender → blob store → CDN → recipient. A view marks the recipient row viewed, fires the read receipt, and enqueues idempotent deletion of the blob plus a CDN purge once the last recipient is done. Store-level TTLs are the safety net. Stories use fan-out-on-read with 24-hour expiry. At 10× resident storage stays bounded; CDN egress dominates.
