Design a Live Streaming Platform (Twitch)

One broadcaster, millions of viewers, and a hard ceiling on how far ahead "live" is allowed to drift. The whole design is a negotiation between latency and scale.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Twitch is YouTube's harder cousin. You can't pre-transcode an upload — the video is being created right now, so ingest, transcode, and delivery all happen on a live clock. The L6/L7 signal is recognizing that latency and scale are in direct tension: the cheap, infinitely-scalable answer (HLS over CDN) costs you seconds of delay, and the low-latency answer (WebRTC) doesn't fan out to millions for free. Staff candidates name that tradeoff out loud, pick per use-case, and don't pretend one transport wins everywhere.[1]

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “What glass-to-glass latency — 2–5 s, or sub-second interactivity?” — Sub-second requires WebRTC; seconds allows CDN-scaled HLS.
  • “How many viewers can one stream have?” — A million-viewer stream makes CDN delivery mandatory.
  • “Do we need DVR rewind and VOD after the stream?” — Storing segments enables both nearly for free.

Functional Requirements

Three flows define the product. Recommendations, follows, subscriptions, payments, and discovery are explicitly out of scope — say so to buy depth on the media path.[1]

#Users should be able to…Why it shapes the design

3Send and read live chat alongside the videoA second real-time fan-out, parallel to the video plane
Out of scope (say it out loud): stream discovery/browse, follows & subscriptions, monetization, clips, moderation tooling, and emote rendering. We also assume one broadcaster per stream — co-streaming and guest invites are a separate problem.[2]

Non-Functional Requirements

This system is defined by its NFRs. The headline tension — low latency vs. massive read fan-out — is the whole interview. State the target, not just the "-ility."

NFRTarget / priority
Low end-to-end latencyGlass-to-glass 2–5s for standard live; < 1s for interactive (auctions, Q&A)[3]
Read scaleMillions of concurrent viewers on a single popular stream; the read:write skew is extreme
Availability over consistencyA dropped chat message or a single skipped frame beats the whole stream stalling
Broadcaster resilienceAbsorb a flaky upstream connection without killing the broadcast (adaptive ingest)
Chat real-timeChat delivered in < 1s, roughly aligned to the video the viewer is seeing
The key tension: "live" is not one latency. A 30M-viewer esports final is happy at 3–5s of delay served from a CDN for pennies; a 50-viewer poker stream taking real-time bets needs sub-second and can afford WebRTC. The transport you pick is a function of the latency budget and the audience size — that's Deep Dive 2, and it's the load-bearing decision.[3]

Say to move on → “The tension is low latency against massive read fan-out. Let me size egress and transcoding.”

Phase 2 · 5–10 minEstimate what mattersOnly the numbers that change a decision — say the implication out loud.

Capacity Estimation

Only compute numbers that change a decision. Two matter: the per-stream egress fan-out, and the transcode cost per concurrent stream. (All figures are my own back-of-envelope estimates.)

  • Egress fan-out (the cost): one popular stream at 5 Mbps × 1M concurrent viewers = 5 Tbps for a single stream. Serving that from origin is impossible and bankrupting — it must come from the CDN edge, exactly like VOD.
  • Transcode cost (per live stream, not per viewer): each ingested stream is transcoded into a ladder of ~5 renditions (1080p→160p) in real time. Cost scales with concurrent broadcasters (~hundreds of thousands), not viewers. This is the part that's unique to live — you pay CPU continuously while the stream is up.
  • Ingest bandwidth: trivial by comparison — one upstream per broadcaster at ~6 Mbps. Ingest is not the bottleneck; delivery is.
  • Chat: a hot stream at thousands of messages/sec × millions of viewers = billions of message-deliveries/sec — its own fan-out problem (Deep Dive 3).
Design implication: two independent scaling axes. Transcode scales with broadcasters; delivery scales with viewers and is owned by the CDN. Never confuse the two.

Interviewer pushback

“Why not stream video from our own servers to viewers?”

Staff answer: A 1M-viewer stream at 5 Mbps is 5 Tbps for one broadcaster. Only CDN edge capacity handles that economically.

API

Derive userId from the auth token, never the body. The video bytes never flow through this API — broadcasters push to an ingest endpoint via RTMP and viewers pull segments straight from the CDN.[1]

  • POST /v1/streams — broadcaster goes live; returns an ingestUrl + secret streamKey for the RTMP push.
  • GET /v1/streams/{id} — returns metadata + manifestUrl (the HLS/DASH playback entry point).
  • GET manifest & GET segments — served by the CDN, not the app tier; the manifest refreshes as new segments land.
  • POST /v1/streams/{id}/chat — send a chat message; userId from JWT.
  • GET /v1/streams/{id}/chat/stream — WebSocket/SSE subscription for live chat (see Deep Dive 3 and Lesson 24).

Data Model

The durable metadata is small; the heavy "data" is a stream of immutable segments addressed by (stream_id, rendition, sequence). The live manifest is just a sliding window over those sequence numbers, a DVR window is a wider slice, a VOD is a frozen range, and a clip is a tiny range — all metadata over the same objects. Stream keys are stored hashed and are rotatable secrets.

Phase 4 · 17–27 minHigh-level designA simple end-to-end design that satisfies every functional requirement.

Data Flow — glass to glass

Live streaming is a pipeline on a real-time clock, so trace the bytes once before drawing boxes:

  1. Capture & encode: broadcaster's software (OBS) encodes camera + screen to H.264/AAC and pushes a continuous RTMP stream to the nearest ingest PoP.[2]
  2. Ingest: an ingest server terminates the RTMP connection, authenticates the streamKey, and hands the raw feed to transcoding.
  3. Transcode: a worker produces a bitrate ladder (e.g. 1080p/720p/480p/360p/160p) in real time and cuts each into 2–6s segments.[4]
  4. Package + manifest: segments are written and an HLS .m3u8 / DASH .mpd manifest is continuously updated with the newest segment URLs.
  5. Distribute: segments + manifest are pushed to the CDN; viewers' players poll the manifest, fetch segments à la carte, and adapt quality. Live chat fans out on a separate real-time plane.

High-Level Design

Three planes, deliberately separated: an ingest/transcode plane (scales with broadcasters), a delivery plane (scales with viewers, owned by the CDN), and a chat plane (its own fan-out). Map each requirement to a component.

ComponentResponsibility
Ingest servers (RTMP)Terminate the broadcaster's push, authenticate the stream key, forward the feed. Geographically close to reduce first-hop latency.[2]
Transcoding fleetReal-time, per-stream: produce the bitrate ladder and cut segments. Autoscaled by concurrent broadcasters.[4]
Packager + OriginWrites segments + live manifest to an origin store; the CDN pulls from here on a cache miss.
CDN (edge)Caches segments + manifests at PoPs near viewers. Absorbs essentially all read bandwidth.[4]
Stream metadata serviceStateless; mints ingest URLs/keys, serves manifestUrl and live/ended status.
Chat service + pub/subPersistent connections fed by a channel-per-stream backplane (see Lesson 24).
DVR/VOD recorderPersists segments to object storage so the stream becomes a replayable VOD (Deep Dive 4).

Watch path: GET /v1/streams/{id} → metadata service → returns manifestUrl → player fetches the live manifest from the CDN → player requests the latest segments from the CDN, adapting quality and re-polling the manifest as new segments appear. The app tier is touched once, for tiny JSON; all heavy bytes flow edge → client — identical to VOD on the read side. (Same CDN + ABR machinery as Lesson 33, YouTube.)

Interviewer pushback

“Why separate chat from video delivery?”

Staff answer: Chat is a real-time fan-out of small messages with different latency and scaling; coupling it to video servers would make a chat surge affect playback.

Phase 5 · 27–47 minDeep dives & tradeoffsGo deep where the system breaks; compare alternatives and justify the pick.

Deep Dives

This is 60% of the interview. Four hard parts, each with the alternative and a justified pick.

Deep Dive 1 — Ingest → real-time transcode → segmented delivery

The broadcaster's encoder (OBS, a console, a phone) pushes a single continuous feed. The standard ingest protocol is RTMP — it's old, TCP-based, and universally supported by encoders, which is exactly why it's still the default contributor protocol.[2] The ingest server authenticates the stream key and hands the feed to a transcoder.

Why transcode at all? The broadcaster sends one quality (say 1080p/6 Mbps), but viewers are on everything from fiber to a phone on the subway. So the transcoder produces a bitrate ladder in real time and cuts the output into short segments (2–6s), generating a continuously-updated HLS/DASH manifest.[4] From the viewer's side this is now identical to VOD: small immutable segments, indexed by a manifest, fetched à la carte and cached at the CDN edge.

ChoicePick & why
Ingest protocolRTMP for contribution — universal encoder support; SRT/WebRTC for lower-latency ingest if needed.[2]
Transcode timingReal-time, per active stream. Unlike VOD you can't pre-process — the content doesn't exist yet. Cost scales with broadcasters.
Delivery formatSegmented HLS/DASH over CDN. Turns "live" into cacheable static objects so delivery scales like VOD.[4]
★ Staff-level signal The reframe that earns the level: once it's segmented, live delivery is just VOD with a sliding window. The genuinely live-only cost is real-time transcode per broadcaster — and you can cap it (transcode fewer renditions for tiny streams, full ladder only for popular ones). State the two scaling axes explicitly: transcode scales with broadcasters, delivery scales with viewers and is the CDN's job. Candidates who try to scale the origin for delivery have missed the problem.

Deep Dive 2 — The latency-vs-scale tradeoff: HLS chunks vs WebRTC

This is the load-bearing decision. Segmented HLS/DASH is what lets one stream reach millions cheaply — but it builds in latency: the player must wait for a segment to be fully written before it can fetch it, and it buffers a few segments to absorb jitter. With 2–6s segments that's commonly 6–15s glass-to-glass.[3] For watching esports that's fine. For an interactive auction or a "guess the next play" bet, it's unusable.

TransportLatencyScaleWhen to use
Standard HLS/DASH~6–15sMillions (pure CDN)Default — large passive audiences
Low-Latency HLS (LL-HLS)~2–4sMillions (CDN, partial segments)The sweet spot for most "live"[3]
WebRTC< 500msHundreds–low thousandsTruly interactive, small rooms

Why WebRTC doesn't just win: it's built for real-time peer-to-peer media, so it gives sub-second latency — but it relies on persistent per-viewer media sessions (SFUs/relays), which don't ride the CDN's cheap static-object caching. Fanning a WebRTC stream out to a million viewers means a fleet of media relays doing per-connection work, which is orders of magnitude more expensive than serving cached segments.[5]

The pick: LL-HLS over CDN as the default — it claws latency down to a few seconds (delivering partial segments before they're complete) while keeping the cacheable, CDN-scalable model. Reach for WebRTC only for the genuinely interactive, small-audience case, and accept that it scales via relays, not the CDN.[3] If a WebRTC stream goes viral, transcode it into the HLS ladder and switch the overflow audience to CDN delivery.
★ Staff-level signal The senior→staff jump is refusing the "one transport wins" trap. Staff says: latency and scale trade off, so pick per use-case. Name the spectrum (WebRTC → LL-HLS → HLS), tie each to an audience size and a latency budget, and propose a hybrid — WebRTC for the interactive core, CDN-backed HLS for the long tail of passive viewers. Relaxing the latency NFR where you can afford to is what makes million-viewer delivery economical.

Deep Dive 3 — Live chat fan-out at scale

Chat is a second real-time fan-out problem, running parallel to the video plane, and it's the same shape as Lesson 24, Facebook Live Comments. A hot stream takes thousands of messages/sec but must deliver each to millions of open connections — so you optimize the read fan-out, not the write.[6]

ConcernApproach
TransportPersistent push — WebSocket (chat is two-way) or SSE; never poll.[6]
Fan-out primitiveChannel per stream over a pub/sub backplane; connection servers subscribe only to channels they serve.
Co-locationL7 LB consistent-hashes on streamId so same-stream viewers cluster on the same connection servers.
Mega-streamSample/curate messages and snapshot a tail buffer to the CDN — nobody reads 1,000 msgs/sec anyway.
Align chat to video, not wall-clock. Because video is delayed several seconds by segmentation, raw real-time chat would spoil moments before the viewer sees them. Tag messages with a stream timecode and let the client render chat roughly in step with the segment it's playing. This "chat lag matches video lag" detail is a strong tell — see Lesson 24 for the full fan-out treatment.[6]

Deep Dive 4 — DVR & VOD recording

Two related features fall out of segmentation almost for free. DVR (pause/rewind a live stream) just means keeping a sliding window of recent segments addressable in the manifest, so the player can seek backward within it. VOD (the stream becomes a permanent replay) means persisting every segment to durable object storage as it's produced.[4]

FeatureHow segmentation gives it to you
DVR / rewindRetain last N minutes of segments; expose them in the live manifest so the player can seek back.
VOD archiveTee every segment to S3 as it's written; on stream end, freeze a VOD manifest over the stored segments.
Clips/highlightsA clip is just a byte-range of segments — generated by referencing existing segments, no re-encode.
★ Staff-level signal The insight: segments are the universal currency. The same immutable chunks serve live playback, DVR rewind, VOD replay, and clipping — you do the expensive transcode once, then everything downstream is metadata over existing bytes. Recognizing that recording is a near-free byproduct of the delivery design (just don't delete the segments) — rather than a separate pipeline — is the staff-level economy of mechanism.

Wrap-up · 58–60 minClose strongRecap decisions and tradeoffs in 60 seconds; name what you would do next.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

A live streaming platform has three planes. Ingest servers accept RTMP from broadcasters and real-time transcoders produce an adaptive-bitrate ladder of short segments. Delivery is LL-HLS through a CDN, so live is just VOD with a sliding window and scales to millions of viewers at 2–4 s latency, with WebRTC reserved for interactive cases. Chat is a separate pub/sub fan-out. Segments are retained for DVR, VOD and clips. At 10× we add CDN capacity and transcoders; egress and transcoding dominate cost.

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. What lets a single live stream reach millions of viewers economically?

Once the live feed is cut into immutable segments, delivery is just VOD on a sliding window — the CDN edge absorbs the read bandwidth.

2. Why isn't WebRTC simply the best transport for every live stream?

WebRTC gives sub-second latency but relies on per-connection relays; it doesn't fan out to millions cheaply, so it fits small interactive audiences.

3. Which scaling axis is unique to live (versus on-demand) video?

You can't pre-transcode content that's being created live, so you pay CPU continuously per active broadcaster; delivery still scales like VOD.

4. How do DVR rewind and VOD replay come almost for free?

DVR keeps a window of recent segments seekable; VOD persists every segment to storage — the expensive transcode happened once, everything else is metadata.
