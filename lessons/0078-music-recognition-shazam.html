Design Music Recognition (Shazam)

Hum the algorithm, not the song — turning a noisy 10-second clip into an audio fingerprint, then finding the one track out of 100M that lines up.

Why this lesson, for your mission: This looks like an ML problem and panics most candidates. The L6/L7 move is to reframe it as a hashing + exact-lookup problem: the magic is a noise-robust fingerprint and an inverted index, not a neural net.[1] Staff signal is naming Avery Wang's combinatorial-hashing trick, defending fingerprint-on-client over uploading raw audio, and knowing the match is decided by a time-offset histogram, not by counting raw hash hits. Get the fingerprint right and the rest is a sharded key-value store you already know.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “Recorded tracks only, or also live versions, covers and humming?” — Exact fingerprinting fails on covers and humming; those need embeddings.
  • “How noisy is the environment and how long a clip?” — Noise tolerance and clip length shape the fingerprint algorithm.
  • “Does fingerprinting run on the device?” — Client-side fingerprints shrink uploads and keep ambient audio private.

Phase 2 · 5–10 minEstimate what mattersOnly the numbers that change a decision — say the implication out loud.

Capacity Estimation

Only estimate the numbers that change a decision. The decision here is "does the index fit in memory on one box, or must we shard?" — so we size the fingerprint index.

Index size (the only number that matters)
  • Catalog: 100M songs × ~3 min each.
  • Fingerprint density: ~20–30 hashes/sec of audio (tunable).[1] Use 25.
  • Per song: 180s × 25 ≈ 4,500 hashes.
  • Total postings: 100M × 4,500 ≈ 4.5 × 10¹¹ (≈ 450B) entries.
  • Each posting ≈ (songID 4B + timeOffset 4B) = 8B, plus the 4B hash key amortized.
  • ≈ 450B × 12B ≈ ~5 TB of index.
Implication: 5 TB will not sit on one machine's RAM. We must shard the index by hash, and we cannot afford a per-song scan — confirming the inverted-index-on-hash design. Storage of the raw audio (req 4) is a separate, cheaper concern handed to a blob store.[3]

Interviewer pushback

“A 5 TB index — why not a single big database?”

Staff answer: Each query multi-gets hundreds of hashes whose postings lists can be long; latency needs the hot index in RAM or on local SSD across shards, scanned in parallel.

Say to move on → “100M songs is ~450B postings, ~5 TB of index sharded by hash. Let me define the API.”

Data Model

The core structure is an inverted index keyed by (algorithm_version, hash) whose value is a packed postings list of fixed-width (song_id, offset) pairs — compact, sequential, and fast to scan. Including the algorithm version in the key lets old and new fingerprint formats coexist during a re-fingerprinting migration. A document-frequency table marks over-common hashes to skip. Song metadata is a small relational table touched only after a match.

Phase 4 · 17–27 minHigh-level designA simple end-to-end design that satisfies every functional requirement.

Data Flow

This is a data-processing system (an ingestion pipeline feeds a query path), so the flow is worth tracing end-to-end:

  1. Ingest (offline): raw track → store in blob store → fingerprinter computes (hash, offset) list → write each posting into the sharded index keyed by hash.[3]
  2. Query (online): client mic → spectrogram → constellation of peaks → hashes → POST /identify → fan out hashes to index shards → gather candidate (songId, offset) postings → time-offset alignment → pick winner → hydrate metadata.

Online: the phone sends hashes; a stateless matcher fans them out to index shards, merges postings, finds the time-offset histogram spike and hydrates metadata. Offline: ingestion workers fingerprint catalog audio with the same algorithm and append postings to the sharded index.

Phase 5 · 27–47 minDeep dives & tradeoffsGo deep where the system breaks; compare alternatives and justify the pick.

Deep Dive 1 — Audio fingerprinting: why it survives noise

You cannot match raw waveforms — two recordings of the same song differ in volume, EQ, codec, and background chatter. The fingerprint must throw away everything except the structure that survives all of that. Avery Wang's insight: work in the spectrogram and keep only the loudest peaks.[1]

  1. Spectrogram. Slice the audio into short windows and FFT each → a time-vs-frequency heatmap of energy.[1]
  2. Constellation of peaks. Keep only local-maximum points (a frequency that is loudest in its neighborhood at that instant). Background noise rarely beats a song's dominant tones, so peaks are robust to noise — the constellation map looks nearly identical clean or noisy.[1]
  3. Combinatorial hashing. A single peak (freq, time) is not distinctive enough. Pair an anchor peak with each peak in a small target zone ahead of it, and hash the triple (freq1, freq2, Δtime). Each pair is far more unique, and pairing makes hashes survive when some peaks are missing.[1]
Why it's noise-robust, in one line: noise adds energy but rarely removes a song's loudest peaks; matching the shape of peak-pairs (not amplitudes) means a 10s clip from a noisy café still produces a subset of the exact same (f1,f2,Δt) hashes the studio track produced.[1] The recording is lossy; the fingerprint degrades gracefully.

Deep Dive 2 — The index & matching by time-offset histogram

The fingerprint index is a classic inverted index: the key is a hash, the value is a postings list of every place that hash occurs.[1]

hash → [(songId, offsetInSong), …]

To identify a clip: for each of its ~100–300 hashes, look up the postings list and collect candidate (songId, songOffset) hits. The naive instinct — "the song with the most matching hashes wins" — is wrong, because popular hashes collide across many songs and noise injects spurious hits.

The real trick is time alignment. If the clip truly came from song X starting at second 47, then every true hash satisfies songOffset − clipOffset ≈ 47 — a constant difference. So per candidate song, build a histogram of (songOffset − clipOffset). A real match produces a sharp spike at one delta (all true hashes agree); coincidental matches scatter uniformly. The winner is the song whose histogram has the tallest spike, and that spike height is your confidence score.[1]

Staff nuance: this is why the match is robust even if 80% of clip hashes are garbage. You're not counting hits — you're detecting a linear correspondence in time. Random hits can't conspire to land on the same delta. This single idea is the heart of the answer.[1]

Deep Dive 3 — Building & ingesting the catalog

Indexing 100M tracks is an embarrassingly-parallel batch job, fully offline and off the read path. The pipeline:

StageWhat happens
LandUpload raw audio to the blob store; enqueue an ingestion job.[3]
FingerprintWorker pulls the file, runs the same spectrogram→peaks→hash code the client uses (one source of truth for the algorithm).
Index writeFor each (hash, offset), append (songId, offset) to that hash's postings list in the sharded index.
MetadataInsert the Song row (title/artist/album) into the metadata DB.

Because ingestion is async and idempotent (key by songId so re-runs overwrite), we can re-fingerprint the whole catalog when we tune the algorithm — a real operational need. Index writes are append-heavy and tolerate eventual visibility, which is exactly why this lives off the latency-critical path.

Deep Dive 4 — Scaling: sharding & approximate matching

From capacity estimation, the index is ~5 TB — it must be sharded by hash. A query's hashes scatter across shards; the matcher fans out, each shard returns its postings, and the matcher merges before running the histogram. Hot hashes (common chords) get huge postings lists — cap/prune the most frequent hashes, which add cost but little discriminating power.

ConcernMove
Index too big for one boxShard by hash; replicate each shard for read throughput.
Hot/over-common hashesDrop or down-weight high-frequency hashes (low signal, high cost).
Exact-hash missesPair-hashing tolerates dropped peaks; quantize Δtime/freq into buckets so near-identical pairs collide.[1]
Humming / cover / fuzzy searchBeyond exact hashing — embed audio as vectors and do ANN. See Lesson 40.

Be explicit about the boundary: Shazam-style recognition is an exact-hash problem and exact lookup is the right tool. Humming a tune or matching a live cover is approximate similarity — there you'd encode audio into embeddings and run approximate-nearest-neighbor against a vector database. Knowing which problem you're in — and not reaching for ML when a hash table suffices — is the judgement being tested.

Deep Dive 5 — Why the client sends a fingerprint, not raw audio

This API choice from §Core Entities deserves defending, because it's the cheap win staff candidates surface unprompted:

DimensionSend raw audioSend fingerprint (chosen)
Bandwidth~150–500 KB for 10s clip~1–3 KB of hashes — cellular-friendly
Server CPUServer must FFT every requestFFT pushed to idle client silicon
LatencyUpload + server computeTiny upload, lookup only
PrivacyAmbient room audio leaves deviceOnly opaque hashes leave device

The cost is a coupling: client and server must run the same fingerprint algorithm, so versioning the fingerprint format becomes a real concern (ship algorithm version in the request; keep server able to match older versions). That tradeoff is worth stating — it's the mark of someone who's shipped, not just designed.

★ Staff-level signal Senior says "fingerprint the audio and look it up." Staff says four things in one breath: (1) "the fingerprint is a constellation of spectrogram peaks hashed in pairs — that's what survives noise, because noise rarely kills the loudest tones"; (2) "matching is decided by a time-offset histogram spike, not by raw hash counts, so 80% junk hashes still match cleanly"; (3) "the index is a sharded inverted index — 100M songs ≈ 5 TB, so it cannot live on one box"; and (4) "this is an exact-lookup problem; I'd only reach for embeddings + a vector DB if the requirement were humming or covers." Picking a hash table over a neural net when a hash table is correct is the simple-by-default judgement L6/L7 rewards.[1]

“Many songs share hashes. How do you find the right one?”

Staff answer: For each candidate song, histogram the difference between the song offset and the clip offset of matching hashes. The true match shows a sharp spike at one offset; random collisions spread out.

Privacy

A microphone feature is privacy-sensitive by nature. By design: the client sends only opaque hashes — ambient conversation never leaves the device and can't be reconstructed from peak-pair hashes. Minimization: recognition history stored only for signed-in users who opt in; anonymous requests keep a hashed device id briefly for abuse control; location coarsened (city-level) if used for charts. Always-on modes (auto-recognition) require explicit consent and visible indicators. Deletion: users can clear history; aggregate charts contain no identities.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Music recognition is an inverted-index lookup with a clever consistency check. The phone turns a noisy clip into a constellation of spectrogram peaks and sends compact peak-pair hashes, never raw audio. Stateless matchers fan hashes out to an index sharded by hash that maps each hash to (song, offset). For candidates, a histogram of offset differences reveals the true match as a sharp spike, and metadata is hydrated from a cached database. An offline pipeline fingerprints the catalog with versioned algorithms. At 10× we add shards and prune common hashes.

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. What makes the fingerprint robust to background noise?

Noise adds energy but rarely removes a song's dominant peaks, so the constellation of peaks looks nearly identical clean or noisy.

2. How does the matcher pick the winning song from candidate hits?

True hashes share a constant (songOffset − clipOffset); that linear time alignment forms a spike. Raw hash counts are misled by collisions.

3. Why must the fingerprint index be sharded across machines?

~4,500 hashes/song × 100M songs ≈ hundreds of billions of postings ≈ ~5 TB — far past one box's RAM, so shard by hash.

4. When would you reach for a vector DB instead of exact hashing?

Exact recognition is a hash-lookup problem. Fuzzy similarity — humming or covers — is approximate search, the job of embeddings + ANN.
