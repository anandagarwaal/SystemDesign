Deep Dive: Consensus (Raft & Paxos)

How a cluster of machines that crash, pause, and lose packets still agrees on one ordered log of truth — and why a simple majority is all the safety you need.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Consensus is the engine inside every coordination service, replicated database, and leader-elected system you'll put on the whiteboard. At L6/L7 the interviewer rarely asks you to implement Raft — they want to see that you understand what consensus guarantees (one agreed log despite failures), why a quorum is the unit of safety ((N/2)+1), and where the boundaries are (2PC is not consensus; consensus can't be both safe and live under a fully async network). Reaching for the right algorithm and naming its cost in one breath is the staff move.[1]

The problem — agreeing despite failure

Consensus is the problem of getting N machines to agree on a single value — and, by extension, on the order of a log of values — even when some of those machines crash, restart, or get cut off by a network partition.[2] Once you can agree on an ordered log, you can build almost anything on top: a replicated state machine where every node applies the same commands in the same order ends up in the same state. That is how a "single" database survives the loss of a node.[2]

The safety trick is the quorum: require any decision to be acknowledged by a majority, (N/2)+1 nodes, before it counts as committed. Any two majorities of the same cluster must overlap in at least one node, and that shared node refuses to accept two conflicting decisions — so the cluster can never commit two different values for the same slot.[2] Majority overlap is the entire reason consensus is safe, and the reason clusters are sized odd: 3 nodes tolerate 1 failure, 5 tolerate 2.

FLP impossibility — the one-line version
  • In a fully asynchronous network (no bound on message delay), no deterministic consensus protocol can guarantee it will always reach a decision if even one node may crash — you cannot tell a dead node from a slow one.[4]
  • The escape hatch: real protocols keep safety unconditionally (they never decide two values) and only rely on timeouts/randomness for liveness (eventually electing a leader once the network behaves). Raft and Paxos both live in this gap.[2]

Raft — leader, log, safety

Raft was designed for one explicit goal: understandability. It decomposes consensus into three sub-problems you can reason about separately — leader election, log replication, and safety — and at any time each node is a follower, candidate, or leader.[2]

Leader election (terms, votes, randomized timeouts)

Time is divided into terms, each a monotonically increasing number that acts as a logical clock.[2] A follower that hears nothing from a leader before its election timeout becomes a candidate, increments the term, votes for itself, and requests votes from everyone else. A node grants its vote to at most one candidate per term, so a candidate that collects a majority of votes becomes leader for that term.[2] The clever part: election timeouts are randomized (e.g. 150–300 ms), so nodes rarely time out at once — split votes are rare and self-correct on the next random timeout, which is how Raft avoids livelock.[2]

Log replication (append, replicate, commit on majority)

Once elected, the leader serves all client writes. Each write becomes a log entry tagged with its term and index; the leader appends it locally, then sends AppendEntries RPCs to replicate it to followers.[2] An entry is committed once the leader has stored it on a majority of nodes — at that point it is durable and the leader applies it to its state machine and replies to the client. The leader tells followers the commit index on subsequent RPCs so they apply the same entries in the same order.[2] The same AppendEntries RPC, sent empty, doubles as the leader's heartbeat to suppress new elections.

Safety (commit rules and log matching)

What stops a stale node from becoming leader and clobbering committed data? Two rules:

  • Election restriction: a node only grants its vote to a candidate whose log is at least as up-to-date as its own. This guarantees the winner already holds every committed entry, so no commit is ever lost.[2]
  • Log Matching Property: if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are identical in all preceding entries. Consistency checks in AppendEntries force followers to converge to the leader's log, overwriting any conflicting uncommitted tail.[2]

A subtle rule Raft is explicit about: a leader only commits entries by counting replicas for an entry from its own current term; older-term entries become committed only indirectly, once a current-term entry above them commits. This closes a window where a committed entry could otherwise be overwritten.[2]

Membership changes

You can't just swap the node set atomically — for a moment two different majorities could form and elect two leaders. Raft uses joint consensus: a transitional configuration requiring majorities from both the old and new sets, so there is never a moment when two disjoint majorities exist.[2] Naming this safe-reconfiguration step is a strong signal that you understand the failure modes, not just the happy path.

Paxos & Multi-Paxos

Paxos solves the same problem and came first; it's the theoretical bedrock but is famously hard to follow.[3] Three roles cooperate (a single process usually plays all three): proposers suggest values, acceptors vote, and learners observe the chosen value.[3] Basic ("single-decree") Paxos chooses one value via two phases:

PhaseWhat happens
1 · PrepareA proposer picks a unique ballot number n and asks a majority of acceptors to promise not to accept anything numbered below n; they reply with any value they've already accepted.[3]
2 · AcceptIf a majority promised, the proposer sends accept(n, v) — where v is the highest-numbered already-accepted value, or its own if none. A majority accepting it makes v chosen.[3]

Running two round-trips per decision is wasteful for a long log. Multi-Paxos amortizes it: elect a stable leader, run Phase 1 once, and then stream a Phase-2-only message per log entry — so the steady state is a single round-trip, just like Raft's AppendEntries.[3] At that point Raft and Multi-Paxos look strikingly similar; Raft's contribution was packaging the leader-based design into something teachable.[2]

2PC is NOT consensus
  • Two-Phase Commit gets participants to agree on commit-vs-abort of a transaction, but it has a single coordinator with no failover. If the coordinator crashes after participants vote "yes," they are stuck holding locks, unable to decide — it blocks.[1]
  • Consensus protocols are exactly the fix: there is no single point of failure because a new leader is elected and the committed log survives any minority of failures. When an interviewer conflates the two, correcting it crisply is high-signal.

★ Staff-level signal Seniors say "I'll use Raft for replication." Staff candidates frame the guarantee and its cost in one breath: "I'll replicate this shard's log with Raft — one leader sequences writes, an entry commits once a quorum of (N/2)+1 has it, so reads are linearizable. That costs me one round-trip of latency per write and means the minority side stops serving writes during a partition, which is the correct CP tradeoff here." And they volunteer the boundaries unprompted: "I'm not hand-rolling consensus — I'll lean on etcd/a Raft library; 2PC wouldn't do because it blocks on coordinator failure; and FLP means I accept it may pause during a bad partition but it will never decide two values."[1] Picking the proven protocol, sizing the quorum, and naming the safety-vs-liveness line is the simple-by-default judgement the bar rewards — without delivering an unasked Raft lecture.

In the interview

You almost never implement consensus on the whiteboard — you invoke it. When your design needs a replicated log, leader election, or a linearizable store, say "this is a consensus group; I'll use Raft (or etcd as a managed one)" and justify it in a line: one leader, quorum commit, linearizable.[2] Be ready for the predictable follow-ups: "Why an odd number of nodes?" (majority overlap; 3 tolerates 1, 5 tolerates 2), "What happens during a partition?" (minority loses quorum, stops writing — CP), and "Why not 2PC?" (it blocks on coordinator failure; consensus elects a new leader). The staff move is depth on demand: name the guarantee and the cost, then move on unless pushed.

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. In Raft, when is a log entry considered committed?

An entry commits once the leader has replicated it to a majority; majority overlap guarantees it survives any minority of failures.

2. Why does Raft use randomized election timeouts across the nodes?

Randomized timeouts mean nodes rarely time out together, so split votes are uncommon and resolve on the next round — avoiding livelock.

3. What does Multi-Paxos optimize compared with basic single-decree Paxos?

A stable leader runs Phase 1 once, then streams Phase-2-only messages per entry, cutting the steady state to one round-trip.

4. Why is classic Two-Phase Commit (2PC) not a consensus protocol?

2PC has one coordinator and no failover, so a coordinator crash leaves participants stuck; consensus elects a new leader and survives.
