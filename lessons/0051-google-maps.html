System Design · Lesson 51 · Phase 4 — Case Study · Hard

Design Google Maps (Navigation)

Render the planet from a CDN, find any place in milliseconds, and route a car across a continent with live traffic — read-heavy everywhere, and precomputed where it counts.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Maps looks like three easy products — a map you pan, a search box, and a blue line A→B — and a junior candidate will treat all three the same. The L6/L7 signal is recognizing that they have completely different shapes: tiles are static reads you push to a CDN, search is a geospatial-index problem you've already solved (Yelp/Uber), and routing is a continent-scale graph shortest-path problem that is impossible at interactive latency without heavy precomputation. Spend your breath on the graph. That's the whole interview.

Data Flow — the live-traffic pipeline

This is the one data-processing path, so it earns a flow. Phones running the app emit anonymized GPS probes; the pipeline turns a firehose of points into one number per road segment: its current travel time.

Probes → ETA, in stages:
  • Ingest. Anonymized, aggregated GPS traces stream in (Kafka). Privacy first — never tie a trace to a user.
  • Map-match. Snap each raw point to the road segment it's actually on.
  • Aggregate. Per segment, compute current median speed → travel time; blend with historical time-of-day patterns when probes are sparse.
  • Publish. Push updated edge costs to the Routing Service's in-memory graph and to the metric-customization step.

The pipeline is async and best-effort: a lagging probe stream degrades to historical estimates, never to an outage. That is the availability NFR made concrete.

Deep Dive 1 — Serving the map: a tile pyramid on a CDN

You never ship "the whole map." The world is sliced into a pyramid of square tiles, one layer per zoom level. At zoom z the map is a 2z × 2z grid of 256×256-px tiles addressed by (z, x, y), projected with Web Mercator [3]. Zoom 0 is one tile for the planet; each zoom-in quadruples the tile count. A viewport only needs the ~dozen tiles it overlaps at its current zoom — so cost scales with screen size, not map size.

ChoiceIdeaVerdict
Render per requestOrigin draws the exact view each call.✗ Can't survive ~700k tiles/s; cache-hostile.
Pre-rendered raster tilesRender every (z,x,y) PNG ahead of time, cache on CDN.~ Trivially cacheable, but petabyte-scale and restyle = re-render everything.
Vector tiles + CDNShip geometry per tile; the client renders and styles it.✓ Far smaller, smooth zoom/rotate, restyle with no re-fetch.

Whichever format, the win is the same: a tile is immutable for a given map version, so it's perfectly cacheable. The CDN serves ~all reads; the origin only renders on a cold miss or a new map version, and you bust the cache by bumping the version in the path. This is a read problem you solve with caching, not a compute problem.

★ Staff-level signal The junior instinct is "render the map." The staff move is reframing tiles as static content addressed by a key — at which point the answer is a CDN, and the only real decisions are vector-vs-raster and how you version for cache-busting. Naming the zoom pyramid (cost scales with viewport, not planet size) and choosing vector tiles to dodge petabyte raster storage shows you see the read/storage tradeoff, not just "use a CDN."

Deep Dive 2 — Routing as a graph problem (the hard part)

Model the road network as a directed weighted graph: nodes are intersections, edges are road segments, and each edge weight is travel time (not distance). "Fastest route" is then single-source shortest path. The trouble is scale: a continent has ~108 nodes, and a route can span thousands of kilometers.

AlgorithmBehavior on a continent graphVerdict
DijkstraExplores an expanding "ball" around the origin until it reaches the target — touches tens of millions of nodes for a long route.✗ Correct but seconds-to-minutes; misses the latency NFR.
A* (with heuristic)Guides search toward the target via a great-circle/lower-bound heuristic.~ Big constant-factor win, but still too much exploration cross-continent.
Precomputation (CH / CRP)Do expensive work offline so each online query touches only thousands of nodes.✓ Millisecond queries at continent scale — the only thing that meets the bar.

Contraction Hierarchies (CH). Offline, order nodes by "importance," then contract them one at a time; whenever removing a node would change a shortest path between its neighbors, add a shortcut edge that preserves the distance [1]. The online query is a bidirectional Dijkstra that only ever relaxes edges going "upward" to more-important nodes — meeting in the middle on the sparse top of the hierarchy. Result: queries explore a tiny fraction of the graph, returning in milliseconds [1].

The catch — and why live traffic complicates CH. CH bakes the edge weights into its shortcuts. If weights change (traffic!), the preprocessing is stale, and rebuilding a full CH is far too slow to run every few minutes. The fix is to separate the slowly-changing topology from the fast-changing metric. Customizable Route Planning (CRP) partitions the graph into nested cells once (topology), and when weights change it runs only a fast customization step that recomputes the costs across cell boundaries — seconds, not hours [2]. That is exactly the shape a traffic-aware planet-scale router needs.

Partitioning into regions

Cut the graph into a multilevel partition of cells, and treat the edges crossing cell boundaries as a small overlay graph. A long route becomes: local search to the origin cell's boundary → a fast hop across the sparse overlay → local search into the destination cell. Partitioning is what makes preprocessing parallelizable and lets a single congested cell be re-customized independently when its traffic shifts [2].

★ Staff-level signal Anyone can say "Dijkstra." The staff answer is the chain: Dijkstra is correct but too slow → A* helps but not enough → so you precompute. Then the decisive insight: live traffic mutates edge weights, so plain CH's baked-in shortcuts go stale. Naming the metric/topology split (CRP: partition once, customize fast) — and that partitioning lets you re-customize only the cells that changed — is the judgement that separates a textbook graph answer from a planet-scale, traffic-aware one.

Deep Dive 3 — Live traffic → edge weights → ETA

ETA is only as good as the edge weights. The Data Flow above turns GPS probes into a current travel time per segment; this deep dive is about using them well.

  • Blend real-time with historical. Where probes are dense, use observed median speed; where sparse (a quiet road at 3 a.m.), fall back to a historical time-of-day model so you always have a number.
  • Push weights into the router. Updated costs flow to the in-memory graph and trigger a CRP customization, so the next query routes around the jam — this is what powers re-routing on an active trip [2].
  • ETA > sum of current edges. A naive ETA adds up current segment times; better is to predict what each segment will look like when you arrive there, using historical curves. The honest interview answer: start simple (sum live edges), then mention predictive ETA as the upgrade.
  • Privacy by construction. Probes are anonymized and aggregated; you publish per-segment speeds, never per-user traces.

Deep Dive 4 — Place search & geocoding

"Find places near me" is the geospatial-index problem you've already met. A B-tree on latitude alone can't co-prune two dimensions, so a radius query degenerates to a scan. Map 2-D into one sortable key with a geohash, quadtree, or S2 cell, then range-scan — identical to the index behind Lesson 20 — Yelp and the nearby-driver lookup in Lesson 29 — Uber.

Search layers two concerns: text match (autocomplete via a prefix/trie or search engine over place names + addresses) and spatial ranking (boost results near the viewport using the geohash/S2 index). Geocoding — turning a typed address into (lat, lng) — sits on the same index. Keep this tight in the interview: it's the breadth part, and the interesting machinery is reused, not new. See the glossary for geohash/S2 definitions.

Privacy

Location traces are among the most sensitive data a company holds. Probe design: probes carry rotating trip tokens (no account id), start/end segments near origins and destinations are trimmed, and raw probes are aggregated per segment then discarded within hours. Aggregation thresholds: a segment's speed is published only when enough distinct probes contribute (k-anonymity), so a lone car on a rural road isn't tracked. Location history (for signed-in users) is opt-in, user-deletable, and stored separately from the traffic pipeline, with auto-delete options. Search queries near a user's location are logged with coarse location only.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Maps splits into three systems. Tiles form a versioned pyramid of immutable vector tiles served from a CDN. Place search uses a geospatial index with text ranking. Routing uses in-memory road graphs preprocessed with contraction hierarchies or customizable route planning, so continent-scale queries finish in milliseconds.
