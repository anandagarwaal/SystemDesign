Choosing a Database

Not "SQL vs NoSQL" — a short sequence of questions about access pattern, consistency, and scale that narrows dozens of options to one defensible pick.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Every case study eventually asks "what do you store this in?" A senior answer names a database. A staff answer runs a small decision procedure out loud — access pattern, then consistency need, then scale and shape — and lands on a pick they can defend against "why not X instead?" This lesson is that procedure, distilled from the individual database deep dives (Postgres, MySQL, Cassandra, DynamoDB, MongoDB, Redis, Elasticsearch, ClickHouse) into one reusable framework.

The order of questions, not a table of products

The mistake is starting from "which database is best" and working backward. The fix is starting from your workload and asking, in order:

#QuestionWhat it eliminates
1What does a typical read/write actually look like?Whether you need joins, range scans, full-text, or a single-key lookup
2What happens if two writes to the same record race?Whether you need strong consistency/transactions or can tolerate eventual
3How much data, and how fast does it grow?Whether a single-node relational DB is enough or you need to shard/go wide-column
4Is this the source of truth, or a derived view?Whether it needs durability/ACID at all, or can be a cache/index rebuilt from the truth

Answer these four, in this order, and the field of "50 databases" collapses to 1–2 defensible candidates before you've named a single product.

Question 1 — access pattern

This is the highest-leverage question because it determines the category, not just the vendor.

Access patternCategoryExample
Lookup by key, simple relations, need joins/transactions across entitiesRelational (SQL)Orders + line items + customers
Lookup by key only, one entity, extreme write throughput, flexible schemaWide-column / key-value (NoSQL)Sensor readings keyed by device_id
Sub-millisecond lookup, ephemeral or semi-durable, rich in-memory structuresIn-memory storeSession state, leaderboard, rate limiter
Full-text search, relevance ranking, faceted filteringSearch indexProduct search, log search
Aggregate over huge historical volumes, few writes, many large scansColumnar / OLAP"Revenue by region by day for 2 years"
Ordered by time, high write rate, queries are always range-by-timeTime-seriesMetrics, IoT telemetry
Similarity search over high-dimensional embeddingsVectorSemantic search, RAG retrieval
One system, one store — usually. Don't reach for a second datastore to solve a problem the first one already solves adequately. A Postgres GIN index handles modest full-text search; you don't need Elasticsearch until relevance ranking or query volume genuinely demands it. Every extra store is an extra thing to keep in sync, monitor, and back up.

Question 2 — consistency and contention

This is the question that decides SQL-family vs. most NoSQL, independent of scale. Ask: "if two writes hit the same row at the same instant, what must happen?"

AnswerNeedFits
Must serialize — money, inventory counts, seat holdsACID transactions, often row-level locking or optimistic concurrencyPostgres/MySQL, or a distributed SQL store (CockroachDB, Spanner) at large scale
Can merge/last-write-wins — profile edits, likes, view countsEventual consistency, tunable per-request if neededCassandra, DynamoDB
Never conflicts by construction — append-only events, logsNo coordination needed; writes are independentKafka-backed log, wide-column store keyed to avoid collisions

This is exactly the AP/CP framing from Replication & CAP, applied one level earlier — before you pick a specific product, decide which side of that line each piece of your data sits on. Most real systems split: strongly consistent for the money/inventory path, eventually consistent for everything else. See flash sale and inventory management for two worked examples of exactly that split.

Question 3 — scale and shape

Only after 1 and 2 does raw size matter, because it decides single-node vs. distributed, not category.

SignalImplication
Fits on one well-provisioned node with room to grow (most systems, even at millions of users)A single primary + read replicas is enough. Don't shard prematurely.
Write volume alone exceeds one node's disk/IOPS, no matter how you tune itShard by a key that matches your access pattern (see Sharding) or move to a store built to shard natively (Cassandra, DynamoDB).
Data is inherently unbounded and append-only (logs, events, metrics)LSM-tree-based stores (Cassandra, ClickHouse) beat B-tree stores on write-heavy ingest.
Read pattern is "one row" vs. "scan millions of rows and aggregate"Row-oriented (Postgres) vs. column-oriented (ClickHouse) storage — the physical layout should match the query shape.
★ Staff-level signal The single most common interview mistake is reaching for a distributed NoSQL store because the numbers in the prompt "sound big." Most numbers that sound big (millions of users, thousands of requests/sec) fit comfortably on a well-tuned single-primary Postgres instance with caching and read replicas [1]. Justify a NoSQL or sharded pick with a specific bottleneck — write throughput past one node's IOPS ceiling, a query shape a relational engine can't serve — not with vibes about "web scale."

Question 4 — source of truth vs. derived view

Not every piece of data needs a durable, ACID-compliant home. Ask what happens if this copy is lost:

If lost…Treatment
Business fact is gone forever — an order, a payment, a stock movementSource of truth: durable, transactional store (relational, or an append-only ledger — see inventory management)
Can be rebuilt from the source of truth — a cache, a search index, a materialized aggregateDerived view: optimize purely for its read pattern (Redis, Elasticsearch, a rollup table), rebuild via CDC if it drifts

This split is why a well-designed system often has three or four different stores and that's not over-engineering: one relational source of truth, one cache for hot reads, one search index for text queries, one CDC-fed aggregate for analytics — each doing the one thing it's good at, none of them the only copy of anything important.

Putting it together

The four-question walk, worked on a real prompt. "Design an inventory system across warehouses."
  • Access pattern: point lookups and small conditional updates by (warehouse, SKU); occasional cross-warehouse aggregation. → Relational for the hot path, a rollup store for aggregates.
  • Consistency: a stock decrement must never lose a concurrent update. → Needs transactions/optimistic concurrency per row — rules out plain eventual-consistency stores for this table.
  • Scale: write volume per warehouse is modest; total volume is large but naturally partitions by warehouse. → Single-node relational per shard, sharded by warehouse_id — no need for a natively-distributed NoSQL store.
  • Source of truth vs. derived: the movement ledger is truth; the cached stock level and the cross-warehouse aggregate are derived and rebuildable. → Postgres (truth) + Redis (cache) + a materialized view (aggregate), wired by CDC.
Four short answers, zero product names mentioned until the end — that ordering is what an interviewer is listening for.

How this shows up in an interview

State the pick once, in the data model section, with a one-line justification per entity ("orders in Postgres — need cross-table transactions; view counts in Redis — high write rate, approximate is fine"). Don't re-litigate it unless the interviewer pushes. If they do, walk the same four questions out loud rather than defending the product name — the questions are the actual signal.

★ Staff-level signal When an interviewer asks "why not just use X instead?", the weak answer defends the chosen product. The strong answer restates which of the four questions X fails on — "DynamoDB would lose the multi-row transaction across the reservation and the order, which this workflow needs." Arguing from the requirement, not from database trivia, is what reads as staff.

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. What should you determine first when choosing a database for a new entity?

Access pattern decides the category (relational, key-value, search, columnar, time-series) before any product or scale question matters.

2. A prompt mentions "millions of users" and a candidate immediately proposes Cassandra. What's the staff-level response?

Most "millions of users" workloads fit on a well-tuned single-primary relational database with caching and replicas; a distributed NoSQL pick needs a concrete bottleneck to justify it.

3. Why might one system reasonably use four different datastores at once?

A relational source of truth plus a cache, search index, and CDC-fed aggregate is not over-engineering — each derived view is rebuildable and optimized for its own query shape.

4. When an interviewer pushes back with "why not X instead," what's the strongest reply?

Arguing from the requirement the alternative fails to meet (not from product trivia) is what demonstrates the reasoning, not just the memorized pick.
