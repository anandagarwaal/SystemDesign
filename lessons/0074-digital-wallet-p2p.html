System Design · Lesson 74 · Phase 4 — Case Study · Hard

Design a Digital Wallet / P2P Payments (Venmo / PayPal)

Hold a balance, move money between two accounts atomically, and prove every cent from an immutable ledger.

Why this lesson, for your mission: A wallet is the purest test of "money correctness" because there is no external provider to blame — you are the source of truth. A senior candidate stores a balance column and updates it; a staff candidate recognizes that a balance is a derived view of an append-only double-entry ledger, that a transfer is an atomic debit+credit, and that concurrent transfers on the same balance will double-spend unless you defend it. The whole bar is reasoning about consistency, atomicity, and idempotency on data where being off by a cent is a bug that ends up in an audit.

This is the wallet/ledger sibling of Lesson 37 (Payment System). That lesson is about integrating an external processor (Stripe) exactly-once. Here the money already lives inside our system, so the focus shifts to balances + a double-entry ledger + internal transfers. We reuse the idempotency and outbox machinery from there, but the hard part is now internal consistency, not the provider.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “Do we hold money in our own ledger, or just move money between banks?” — A stored-value wallet needs a double-entry ledger as source of truth.
  • “Are transfers instant between users of the same wallet?” — Instant internal transfers need atomic debit and credit.
  • “Which funding sources and cash-out methods?” — Cards and ACH add external settlement, fees and reversals.

Functional Requirements

Scope tightly to what exercises the hard parts: holding value and moving it between two of our own accounts.

#Requirement
1A user can check their balance — see current available funds in their wallet.
2A user can add money to their wallet (a top-up that credits the wallet from a funding source).
3A user can transfer money (P2P) to another user — an atomic debit of the sender and credit of the receiver.
4A user can view transaction history — a chronological list of debits and credits on their wallet.

Out of scope (name it, then drop it): card-network/processor integration (that's Lesson 37), currency conversion / FX, interest & lending, KYC onboarding, and fraud scoring. We keep add money as a thin credit entry rather than re-deriving the whole processor flow.

Non-Functional Requirements

A wallet inverts the usual priority order, just like payments: correctness beats durability beats availability beats latency. It is always better to reject a transfer than to let a balance go wrong.

PropertyTarget / priority
Correctness / consistencyHighest. No double-spend, no money created or destroyed. Every transfer is all-or-nothing (debit and credit both commit or neither does).
Durability & auditabilityNo committed transaction is ever lost; every money movement is permanently recorded and re-derivable for audit.
AvailabilityHigh for reads (balance, history); writes may briefly reject under contention rather than risk a wrong balance.
LatencySoft: a transfer should confirm in well under a second. Acceptable to serialize writes per account to protect correctness.

The headline tension: money writes want strong consistency (a single, agreed-upon balance), which fights the availability/latency story you'd reach for in a feed. See Lesson 06 (Replication & CAP) — for money you pick C over A on the write path.

Phase 2 · 5–10 minEstimate what mattersOnly the numbers that change a decision — say the implication out loud.

Capacity Estimation

Estimate only the numbers that change the design.

  • Throughput: assume ~100M users, ~10 wallet ops/user/month ≈ ~1B ops/month ≈ ~400 writes/s average, ~4K writes/s at peak. Modest — the bottleneck is not aggregate QPS, it's per-account contention on hot wallets (a merchant, a giveaway), which is what forces the concurrency deep dive.
  • Storage: double-entry means ≥2 ledger rows per transfer. ~1B transfers/month × 2 rows × ~0.3 KB ≈ ~600 GB/month of ledger, growing forever (append-only). Cheap per row, but it compounds — drives partitioning by account and archival of cold history.
  • Retention: financial records are kept for years (often 7+ for compliance) and are never mutated or deleted. This alone forces an append-only ledger with snapshotting rather than an in-place balance column.

Design implications: aggregate load is small, so we optimize for per-account correctness under contention and for an immutable, ever-growing, auditable ledger — not for raw QPS.

Phase 3 · 10–17 minAPI & data modelLock the contract and the storage shape before drawing boxes.

Core Entities & API

Three entities carry the weight. The caller's identity comes from the auth token, never the request body.

  • Account / Wallet — one per user. Holds accountId, ownerId, currency, and a snapshotted balance + version (the balance is a cache of the ledger, not the truth).
  • LedgerEntry — an immutable debit or credit: (transferId, accountId, direction, amount, createdAt). Append-only; the source of truth for "what happened".
  • Transfer — one logical money movement linking exactly two entries (one debit, one credit) that must sum to zero, with a client idempotencyKey and a status.

Data Model

The ledger is the truth and the account row is a versioned snapshot of it. Each ledger entry records the account's balance_after and a per-account sequence, so any balance can be verified by walking entries and gaps are detectable. A CHECK (balance_minor >= 0) on the snapshot is a database-level backstop against double-spend, and a unique idempotency key per sender makes retries return the original transfer. Cross-shard transfers add a holds table for the saga's reserved funds.

A same-shard transfer is one ACID transaction: idempotency check, funds check, debit entry, credit entry, snapshot updates and outbox row. Transfers whose accounts live on different shards run as a saga (hold on the sender, credit the receiver, confirm or compensate). Reconciliation continuously re-derives balances from entries and checks every transfer nets to zero.

High-Level Design

Request flow for POST /v1/transfers: the client hits the Wallet API behind a load balancer. The service first checks the idempotency store for the key; if seen, it returns the stored result. Otherwise, inside a single ACID transaction on the ledger DB, it: (1) verifies the sender has sufficient funds, (2) appends a debit entry to the sender and a matching credit entry to the receiver, (3) updates both account snapshots (guarded by a version check), and (4) records the transfer + idempotency key. Either all of that commits or none of it does. Balance reads and history reads go straight to the snapshot / ledger and can be served from read replicas.

Deep Dives

1 · The double-entry ledger as the source of truth

The single most important decision: the balance is not a column you mutate; it is a value you derive. Money lives as an append-only, immutable stream of double-entry ledger entries — every transfer writes at least two entries, a debit on one account and an equal credit on another, and the entries of a transfer must sum to zero.[2] Nothing is ever updated or deleted in place; a correction is a new compensating entry, not an edit.

Why money demands this (and strong consistency):
  • Auditability. Every cent traces to a transfer; you can replay history to recompute any balance at any past instant.
  • Conservation. If all entries sum to zero, money is never silently created or destroyed — bugs become visible imbalances.
  • Tamper-evidence. Append-only means a changed past entry breaks the running totals; you can't quietly rewrite history.
  • Strong consistency on writes. A balance check that decides "do you have enough?" must read a single agreed-upon truth. An eventually-consistent/stale read here lets two transfers both see "enough" and overspend.

Re-summing the entire ledger on every balance read is too slow, so we keep a snapshot: a materialized running balance per account (a row, or periodic checkpoint + recent entries). The snapshot is an optimization; the ledger remains the truth, and reconciliation re-derives the snapshot from entries to prove they agree. This is the same append-only, derive-don't-mutate discipline you saw in Lesson 37, and it rests on the transactions/consistency fundamentals in Lesson 06.

2 · A transfer as an atomic debit + credit (idempotent)

A P2P transfer is two writes that must be all-or-nothing: debit sender, credit receiver. If only one lands, money vanishes or is conjured. Because both accounts are in our DB, the clean answer is a single ACID transaction wrapping both entries + both snapshot updates — atomicity comes for free from the database.[1] This is the wallet's advantage over the external-provider case.

When sender and receiver live on different shards/databases (you partition by account), one local transaction no longer spans both. Then you orchestrate a two-phase / saga flow: pending debit on the sender (funds reserved) → credit the receiver → complete; if the credit fails, a compensating entry refunds the sender. This is the saga pattern from Lesson 10 (Common Patterns).

Either way, retries must not double-charge. The client sends an idempotency key per logical transfer; on arrival the API does a conditional insert keyed on it, so the first request executes and any retry returns the stored result instead of moving money twice[1] — the exact discipline from Lesson 37.

3 · Concurrency on a single balance — locking vs. optimistic

The classic bug: two transfers from the same wallet run at once, both read balance = $100, both think the $80 they want to send is covered, both commit — now the balance is −$60. This is a lost-update / double-spend race. Two standard defenses:

StrategyHowBest for
Pessimistic lockSELECT ... FOR UPDATE the account row; the second transfer blocks until the first commits.Hot accounts with real contention; guarantees serial debits.
Optimistic (version)Read balance + version; on write, UPDATE ... WHERE version = N; if 0 rows, retry.Low-contention accounts; avoids holding locks, but retries under load.

The staff move is to pick per access pattern: optimistic for the long tail of cold wallets, pessimistic (or a serialized per-account queue) for hot ones. Either way the funds check and the debit happen in the same transaction at a serializable-enough isolation level, so "check then act" can't interleave. A pure stale read from a replica must never gate a debit — that's the strong-consistency requirement from deep dive 1 in action.

4 · Exactly-once, reconciliation & security

Exactly-once. At-least-once delivery (clients/queues retry) plus idempotent processing (the key) equals effectively exactly-once — the same equation as Lesson 10. The transfer state machine (pending → completed | failed, terminal states) makes illegal re-execution impossible.

Reconciliation. A periodic job re-sums every account's ledger entries and asserts (a) the recomputed total equals the stored snapshot, and (b) every transfer's entries net to zero across the system. Any drift is flagged for investigation[2] — the append-only ledger is what makes this diff trustworthy.

Security. Always derive the sender from the auth token, never the body, or one user drains another's wallet. Authorize that the caller owns the source account. Enforce idempotency to stop replayed transfer requests, validate amounts are positive and within limits, and rate-limit transfers to blunt abuse — these are the standard input-trust and authorization rules, applied to money.

★ Staff-level signal Four moves separate staff from senior here: (1) state up front that the balance is derived from an append-only double-entry ledger, not a mutable column, and that money writes need strong consistency; (2) model a transfer as an atomic debit+credit — one ACID transaction same-shard, a saga with compensation cross-shard; (3) name the concurrent double-spend race unprompted and choose locking vs. optimistic per access pattern; (4) add idempotency keys + reconciliation as the exactly-once and audit backstop. Bonus: saying you'd rather reject a transfer than corrupt a balance, and reusing — not re-deriving — the payment system patterns.

Privacy

A wallet holds regulated financial PII: identity (KYC documents), bank links, balances, and a social graph of who pays whom (with memos that often reveal sensitive details). Defaults: transaction feeds private by default (a lesson learned from public P2P feeds). Encryption: bank account numbers and KYC documents field-level encrypted; tokenized funding sources. Retention vs erasure: ledger records retained for years under financial regulations, so erasure means pseudonymizing profile data while keeping entries. Access: staff views masked by default; unmasking audited. Sharing: no selling transaction data; regulatory reporting (suspicious activity) only as required.

Wrap-up · 58–60 minClose strongRecap decisions and tradeoffs in 60 seconds; name what you would do next.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

A digital wallet is a double-entry ledger with a cached balance. Every money movement writes balanced, immutable debit and credit entries. A transfer checks an idempotency key, then in one ACID transaction on the sharded ledger database locks or conditionally updates the sender's balance version, writes both entries and updates the balances. Double-spending and negative balances become impossible. Cross-shard transfers use holds and a saga. Daily reconciliation proves ledger totals against bank settlements. At 10× we add shards by account.
