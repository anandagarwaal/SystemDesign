Design a Price Tracking / Alerting Service

Like CamelCamelCamel: poll millions of products, and fire the right alert the moment a price drops.

Why this lesson, for your mission: This problem looks like a CRUD app and isn't. The L6/L7 signal is recognizing that "which alerts fire when this price changes?" is the whole interview — and inverting it so a price update does a cheap range lookup instead of scanning every tracker. Get that, plus a queue-backed ingestion pipeline and idempotent notifications, and you've shown you can find the bottleneck before you've drawn a single box.

Functional Requirements

Scope down to the two capabilities that define the product, and defer the rest.[1]

#A user should be able to…
1Track a product and set a target price (a threshold).
2Be notified when the tracked product's price drops to or below their target.
3View a product's price history (the chart).

Out of scope (say this out loud): product search/discovery, multi-retailer comparison, reviews. We poll prices from a source; we don't model the merchant.[1]

Capacity Estimation

Only the numbers that change a decision. Anchor on the real CamelCamelCamel scale.

  • Products: ~18M tracked at a 6-hour poll interval is the public figure;[2] design for 500M as the stretch target.[1]
  • Price-check rate: 18M ÷ 6h ≈ 830 checks/sec sustained. At 500M and 6h that's ~23k/sec — this is the pipeline's load, and it sets worker-pool size.
  • Price records: ~72M new price points/day → ~26B records/year.[2] At ~50 bytes/point that's ~1.3 TB/yr raw before compression → time-series store, not a B-tree.
  • Alert evaluation: a single price change may match anywhere from 0 to thousands of trackers. The design must make the common case (0 matches) nearly free.
Takeaways: the write path (ingestion) and the match path are the two scaling problems; user-facing reads are cache-friendly and easy.

Data Flow

This is a pipeline, so trace the lifecycle of one price change end to end before drawing the HLD:

  1. Scheduled fetch — the scheduler enqueues "check product X" jobs at its poll interval.
  2. Ingest & persist — a worker fetches the current price, appends a PricePoint, updates the product's current price.
  3. Compare against thresholds — if the price dropped, ask: "which trackers on X have targetPrice ≥ newPrice?"
  4. Match → alert events — each matching, non-cooled-down tracker emits an alert event onto a queue.
  5. Notify — the notification service fans out to email/SMS/push, idempotently, and stamps lastNotifiedAt.

Steps 1–2 are the ingestion problem; step 3 is the matching problem; step 5 is the fan-out problem. Each gets a deep dive.

Deep Dive 1 — Inverting the match (the staff move)

The naive read: when product X's price changes, load every tracker, check each one. At 500M products with thousands of trackers on hot items, a per-change full scan is fatal. Invert the question.

You don't iterate trackers; you query them. Maintain a composite index (productId, targetPrice). A price drop to newPrice becomes a single range query:

SELECT id, userId FROM trackers
WHERE productId = X AND targetPrice >= newPrice AND active = true;

The index seeks straight to the matching slice. The common case — a price that didn't cross anyone's threshold — returns zero rows in log time, touching almost nothing.[2]

Two refinements interviewers reward:

  • Only evaluate on a drop. If the new price ≥ old price, no targetPrice ≥ newPrice tracker that wasn't already satisfied becomes newly satisfied — skip the query entirely.
  • Avoid re-firing. A tracker that already alerted shouldn't fire again next poll while the price stays low. Mark it (drop from the "armed" set, or check lastNotifiedAt) so the range query returns only newly-crossed trackers, not everything currently below target.
★ Staff-level signal Saying "I'll index trackers by (productId, targetPrice) and turn a price change into a range query instead of scanning" is the single highest-leverage sentence in this interview. It shows you reframed an O(trackers) loop into an O(log n + matches) lookup — the exact judgement that separates senior from staff. Bonus: note that for the hottest products this index slice fits in memory, so the match is effectively free.

Deep Dive 2 — The ingestion / scheduling pipeline

You must poll millions of products politely without melting the source. Two collection models:

ModelProsCons
Push (source webhooks/feed)Real-time, no polling waste.Rarely available for third-party retail; you don't control it.
Poll (official API + scrape)Works against any retailer; you set cadence.Wasteful (most checks see no change); rate-limited (~1 req/s/account on the official API).[2]

The answer is poll, decoupled through a queue:

Scheduler → Queue → Worker pool
  • The scheduler enqueues per-product check jobs on a cadence — and varies it: hot/trending products every ~30 min, the long tail every 6–12h.[2] This concentrates effort where prices actually move.
  • The queue decouples scheduling from execution and absorbs Prime-Day bursts; workers scale horizontally and self-pace, partitioned by ASIN range/category.[2]
  • Politeness: per-source rate limiting, proxy/IP rotation, exponential backoff + retries; failures land in a dead-letter queue.[2]
  • Batch fetches and writes — group products to amortize connection and write overhead.[1]

Why a queue and not the scheduler calling workers directly? Backpressure. If fetches slow down, jobs buffer in the queue instead of overwhelming workers or the source — and you can replay after an outage.

Deep Dive 3 — Notification fan-out, dedup & idempotency

Matched alerts go onto a queue and the notification service fans out to email/SMS/push. The hard requirement is "don't alert twice" while the transport is at-least-once (retries can redeliver).

  • Idempotency key. Stamp each alert event with a deterministic key, e.g. hash(trackerId + crossingId). The notifier records sent keys; a redelivered event with a seen key is dropped. At-least-once delivery + dedup = effectively-once.[2]
  • Cooldown. A price oscillating around the threshold would fire on every poll. A 24-hour cooldown per tracker (or "fire once, then re-arm only after the price recovers above target") kills alert fatigue.[2]
  • Reliable delivery. Retries with backoff; persistent failures → dead-letter queue for inspection, never silently dropped.[2]
  • Fan-out across channels via provider adapters (SES, Twilio, FCM), each with its own rate limits and retry policy.[2]
★ Staff-level signal Volunteering the idempotency key before being asked "what if it sends twice?" signals you treat at-least-once queues as the default and design dedup in, rather than hoping delivery is exactly-once (it never is).

Deep Dive 4 — Storing & scaling price history

26B append-only points/year, read as time-ranges to draw charts. This is a textbook time-series workload, not a relational one.[1]

TechniqueWhat it buys
Partition by productIdAll of a product's history colocates → one-seek range scans.[2]
Columnar compression10–20× shrink on numeric price data.[2]
Tiered storageHot SSD for recent ~90d, cold object store (S3/Glacier) for the archive.[2]
Downsample old dataDaily/weekly aggregates for old history cut ~90% of storage and speed long-range reads.[2]
Server-side downsamplingAn 800px chart needs ≤800 points — return aggregates, not raw rows.[2]
Cache hot productsRedis keyed by product+range; 90%+ hit rate on popular items.[2]

Keep product/user metadata in Postgres; keep the firehose of price points in the time-series store. Splitting them lets each scale on its own axis — the kind of "right tool per workload" judgement L6/L7 expects.

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. A price change arrives. What is the efficient way to find which trackers fire?

Indexing on (productId, targetPrice) turns a full scan into a log-time range lookup.

2. Why sit a queue between the scheduler and the fetch workers?

The queue gives backpressure and replay; workers self-pace instead of overwhelming the source.

3. Delivery is at-least-once. How do you avoid alerting a user twice?

At-least-once plus an idempotency key (per crossing) gives effectively-once delivery.

4. Why store price history in a time-series store instead of a normal SQL table?

26B points/year compress ~10–20×, partition by product, and downsample for fast chart ranges.
