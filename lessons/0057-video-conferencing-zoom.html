System Design · Lesson 57 · Phase 4 — Case Study · Hard

Design Video Conferencing (Zoom / Google Meet)

Real-time media is the opposite of the systems you've practiced: you throw data away on purpose, you prefer UDP over TCP, and the server's job is to forward — not to store.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Conferencing is the case study that breaks request/response instincts. At L6/L7 the bar is not "draw a media server" — it is to separate the signaling plane from the media plane, justify WebRTC over UDP for sub-150ms latency, and reach for an SFU topology that scales group calls without melting the client's uplink. Name those three moves with their tradeoffs and you've shown staff-level judgement; over-explain WebSockets and you've shown senior.

Phase 1 · 0–5 minClarify the problemPin scope and the few NFRs that will drive every later decision.

Ask before you design

  • “What's the maximum meeting size?” — Topology choice (mesh, SFU, MCU) depends on participants per room.
  • “Do we need recording and live transcription?” — Recording needs a server-side participant that composes streams.
  • “Do we need to support phone dial-in or low-power devices?” — Legacy endpoints may require transcoding (MCU-style) gateways.

Functional Requirements

Scope tightly to the live-call core. Say the deferrals out loud — it signals you know the surface area without drowning in it.

#Users should be able to…
1Create and join a meeting via a shareable ID/link, with a lobby and host controls.
2Exchange real-time audio and video with everyone else in the room.
3Share a screen (a second, high-resolution video track) to the whole room.
4Have calls work with many participants (1:1 up to large group rooms).

Out of scope (state it): chat, reactions, breakout rooms, virtual backgrounds, transcription/AI, and webinar-scale (10k+ view-only) broadcast — that's the live-streaming problem from Lesson 56, not conferencing.

Phase 2 · 5–10 minEstimate what mattersOnly the numbers that change a decision — say the implication out loud.

Capacity Estimation

Estimate only the numbers that change the topology. For conferencing the load-bearing figure is egress bandwidth per room, which is exactly what separates mesh, MCU, and SFU.

  • Per-stream bitrate: a 720p video track ≈ 1–2 Mbps; audio ≈ 40 kbps. Use ~1.5 Mbps/video as the working number.
  • The killer: client uplink in a mesh. In an N-person room with no server, each client sends its stream to the other N−1 peers. At N=10 that's ~9 × 1.5 = ~13.5 Mbps upload per person — most home connections die here. This single number kills mesh for groups.
  • SFU egress: each client uploads once (~1.5 Mbps); the server fans out. Server egress per room ≈ N × (N−1) × 1.5 Mbps. A 10-person room ≈ ~135 Mbps egress on one media node — so media servers are bandwidth-bound, and you pin a room to a region near its participants.
  • Signaling vs media: signaling is a trickle (a few KB at join, then heartbeats) — handled by ordinary stateful WebSocket servers like Lesson 19's chat tier. Media is the firehose and lives on a totally separate fleet.

Implication: the design is decided by who pays the fan-out cost. Move it off the client (mesh fails) and off a transcoder (MCU is CPU-bound) and onto a forwarding server (SFU) — that's the whole case study in one sentence. Bitrates are standard WebRTC ballparks; treat as my own estimates.

Core Entities & API

Entities: User, Meeting (room id, host, settings), Participant (user + device + connection state + assigned media node), and MediaStream / track (audio, camera, screen). A Recording entity exists only if recording is on.

Split the API by plane. The control plane (create/join, negotiate) is plain REST + a signaling WebSocket — it's fine to be TCP-reliable because it's tiny and infrequent. The media plane is not an API you call; it's WebRTC peer connections to a media server, negotiated through signaling.

High-Level Design

The defining move is the two-plane split: a thin, reliable signaling plane that sets calls up, and a fat, lossy media plane that carries the actual audio/video and never touches your database on the hot path.

Deep Dives

1. Why WebRTC over UDP — and why TCP is wrong for live media

The instinct from every other lesson is "use TCP, it's reliable." For real-time media that reliability is actively harmful. TCP guarantees in-order, lossless delivery, which means a single lost packet causes head-of-line blocking: everything behind it waits for the retransmit. In a conversation, a video frame that arrives 300ms late is useless — the moment has passed — yet TCP would stall the whole stream to redeliver it.

Real-time media wants the opposite contract: send fast, tolerate loss, never wait. That's UDP. WebRTC builds on UDP and layers in exactly what media needs — RTP for timestamped/sequenced media packets, RTCP for feedback (loss reports, bitrate hints), DTLS-SRTP for mandatory encryption, and ICE/STUN/TURN for connectivity [2]. A dropped packet becomes a tiny glitch the codec conceals, not a freeze. TCP (and TURN-over-TCP) is kept only as a fallback for hostile networks that block UDP.

★ Staff-level signal Say the sentence: "For live media, latency beats reliability, so a late packet is worthless and resending it makes things worse." A senior reaches for TCP/WebSocket reflexively; a staff engineer explains why head-of-line blocking makes reliability a liability here, then names UDP + RTP + a jitter buffer as the correct stack. Knowing when not to want a guarantee is the judgement being tested.

2. Topology: mesh vs MCU vs SFU — and why SFU wins group calls

This is the heart of the design. Three ways to get N participants' streams to each other, each pushing the fan-out cost to a different place.

TopologyHow it worksCost / verdict
Mesh (P2P)Every client sends its stream directly to every other client; no media server.Client uploads N−1 copies → uplink explodes (~13.5 Mbps at N=10). Fine for 1:1–3, dies for groups [3].
MCU (Multipoint Control Unit)Server decodes all streams, composites them into one mixed stream, re-encodes, sends one stream down.Client uploads/downloads once (cheap client), but server decode+encode is hugely CPU-bound → expensive, adds latency. Legacy.
SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit)Server receives each stream and forwards packets as-is to the others. No decode, no re-encode [3].Client uploads once; server is bandwidth-bound but cheap on CPU; per-receiver layer selection. The winner for groups.

The SFU wins because it moves fan-out off the client (fixing mesh's uplink) without paying the MCU's transcoding bill. The client sends one upstream; the SFU does the N×(N−1) replication on fat datacenter pipes. The tradeoff: each receiver now downloads N−1 streams, which the next deep dive controls with simulcast and active-speaker selection. Modern Zoom/Meet/most WebRTC platforms are SFU-based.

3. Making the SFU scale: simulcast, adaptive layers, and jitter buffers

A raw SFU forwarding every stream at full quality still overwhelms weak receivers. Three mechanisms fix it:

  • Simulcast: each sender encodes its camera at multiple resolutions/bitrates simultaneously (e.g. 1080p/360p/180p) and uploads all of them. The SFU then forwards the layer that fits each receiver's bandwidth — a strong viewer gets HD, a weak one gets the low layer, from the same upload [3]. (SVC is the codec-level variant: one layered stream.)
  • Adaptive selection / active speaker: the SFU doesn't forward all N at HD. It sends the active speaker in high res and others as thumbnails or audio-only, reacting to RTCP feedback and bandwidth estimates. This is how a 50-person room stays watchable.
  • Jitter buffer: UDP packets arrive out of order and with variable delay (jitter). The receiver holds a tiny buffer (tens of ms) to reorder and smooth playout, trading a sliver of latency for stability. Late packets are dropped, not awaited; packet-loss concealment masks the gap.
★ Staff-level signal The staff insight is that quality adaptation is the receiver's problem solved with the sender's help: the sender does cheap extra work (simulcast layers) so the SFU can make a per-receiver choice without any transcoding. A senior proposes "the server downscales for weak clients" (re-inventing the MCU's CPU cost); a staff engineer pushes the encode to the edge (sender) and keeps the SFU a dumb, fast forwarder. Cost lives where it's cheap.

4. Signaling vs media planes, NAT traversal, and recording

Two planes, two transports. Signaling carries SDP offers/answers and ICE candidates — small, infrequent, and it must be reliable, so it rides a TCP WebSocket. Media carries the firehose — large, continuous, loss-tolerant — so it rides UDP/RTP straight to the SFU. They are different fleets with different scaling laws: signaling scales like Lesson 19's connection servers; media scales by bandwidth and region. Conflating them is a classic senior flattening.

NAT traversal (STUN/TURN). Most clients sit behind NAT with no routable public address, so peers can't just dial each other. WebRTC uses ICE to gather candidate paths: STUN lets a client discover its own public IP:port so a direct path can often be punched through [4]; when symmetric NATs or firewalls block that (~10–20% of the time), TURN relays the media through a server as a fallback — reliable but it costs you bandwidth and a hop of latency [4]. With an SFU the "peer" is the SFU itself, which has a public address, so traversal is mostly client↔SFU and simpler than full mesh P2P.

Recording. Don't bolt it onto the hot path. Add a headless participant ("ghost" client / recording bot) that subscribes to the SFU like any other receiver, composites the layout, and writes to object storage asynchronously — server-side so it survives clients leaving. Keep it off the live forwarding path so it can never add latency to the call.

Privacy

Conversations are highly sensitive. Encryption: media is always encrypted in transit (DTLS-SRTP), but a standard SFU terminates hop-by-hop encryption; end-to-end encryption (insertable streams / SFrame) keeps keys on clients so the SFU forwards ciphertext it can't decode — at the cost of server-side features (cloud recording, transcription, dial-in). Consent: recording requires visible indicators and notice to all participants. Retention: recordings auto-delete per org policy; quality telemetry keeps participant ids pseudonymized. Residency: route media through SFUs in the customer's chosen region. Minimization: the media plane stores nothing by default.

Wrap-up · 58–60 minClose strongRecap decisions and tradeoffs in 60 seconds; name what you would do next.

Closing Summary (60 seconds)

Video conferencing splits a reliable signaling plane from a lossy real-time media plane. Clients join through signaling servers that authenticate them and assign a regional SFU. Media travels over WebRTC and UDP with ICE, STUN and TURN for NAT traversal. Senders simulcast multiple layers, and the SFU forwards each receiver the layer its bandwidth supports, with no transcoding. That keeps client uplink constant and server CPU low. Large rooms cascade across SFUs, and recording joins as a server-side participant. At 10× we add SFUs and edge regions.
