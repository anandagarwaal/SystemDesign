Design a Food Delivery App (DoorDash / UberEats)

Three actors, one order that crosses three systems, and a menu read path that dwarfs everything else by orders of magnitude.

Why this lesson, for your mission: Food delivery is the three-sided marketplace that pulls together everything Phase 4 has taught — the geospatial matching of Uber, the read-heavy/strong-write split of Gopuff, and the live-tracking of any realtime system. The breadth is deceptively easy to draw. The L6/L7 signal lives in four deep dives: dispatching a courier to an order as a geospatial-plus-optimization problem, holding the order together as a saga across restaurant, courier, and payment, serving menus at extreme read scale, and pushing live tracking to three phones at once. Name those tradeoffs and the rest is plumbing.

Functional Requirements

Three actors — customer, restaurant, courier — but you scope to the customer's order journey. Four flows carry the whole interview; loyalty, ratings, promotions, and group orders are non-goals you name and defer.

#Users should be able to…
1Browse nearby restaurants and view a menu — the discovery surface, filtered by deliverable location.
2Place an order from a single restaurant and pay for it, atomically.
3Have the platform match a courier to the order and dispatch them to pick up and deliver.
4Track the order and courier live — status (placed → preparing → picked up → delivered) and the courier's position on a map.

Non-Functional Requirements

State a target for each — vague "scalable and reliable" reads as junior. These are the constraints the deep dives keep returning to.

NFRTarget & why it shapes the design
Low-latency menu readsBrowse and menu render in < 200ms p99. Reads dominate by ~100×; forces caching + CDN, not per-request DB joins.
High availability at peakDinner rush (Fri/Sat 6–8pm) is multiples of average. Browse/track must degrade gracefully, never go dark. Availability > perfect freshness for reads.
Consistency on orders & paymentStrong. An order is charged once, never double-charged, never silently lost. This is the strong-consistency island in an availability-first system.
Dispatch latencyCourier assigned within seconds of the restaurant accepting. Forces an in-memory geo index — no table scan per order.
Per-city scalabilityA delivery is intrinsically local. Geo-shard so a NYC dinner spike never touches Chicago's capacity.

Capacity Estimation

Only compute numbers that change a decision. The headline here is the asymmetry between menu reads and order writes.

The read/write gap. Assume ~20M orders/day. That's 20M ÷ 86,400s ≈ ~230 orders/sec average, perhaps ~1–2k/sec at dinner peak. But every order is preceded by heavy browsing — say ~50 menu/restaurant-list reads per order placed (people window-shop). Menu reads land near ~12k/sec average, ~50k+/sec at peak[1].
  • Implication of ~50k menu read QPS: a relational join per request would melt the primary. Menus are read-mostly and change rarely — push them to a cache + CDN and serve precomputed blobs (Deep Dive 3).
  • Implication of ~1–2k order write QPS: orders are low volume. A single strongly-consistent primary can absorb writes, so you can afford an expensive multi-step transaction / saga on the order path (Deep Dive 2).
  • Implication of courier pings: ~1M active couriers at peak pinging every ~4s → ~250k location writes/sec. This is a firehose — keep it in an in-memory geo index, never a durable table (mirrors Uber).

The numbers are the thesis: read-heavy menus, light-but-correct orders, a courier-location firehose. Three load profiles, three different storage choices.

Data Flow

The order's lifecycle crosses three independent systems, so it helps to trace it once end-to-end before drawing boxes:

1. Discover → customer browses nearby restaurants (cache/CDN), opens a menu (cache/CDN).
2. Order → POST /orders creates the order in PENDING, charges payment, notifies the restaurant.
3. Accept → restaurant confirms and gives a prep-time ETA → order goes CONFIRMED.
4. Dispatch → matching service finds a nearby available courier and assigns them (timed to arrive as food is ready).
5. Fulfill → courier picks up (PICKED_UP), delivers (DELIVERED); each transition pushes to the customer's tracking socket.

Steps 2–5 are a distributed transaction spanning payment, restaurant, and courier — which is exactly why the order needs a saga, not a single DB transaction (Deep Dive 2).

High-Level Design

Split the system along its three load profiles: a read path (menus/browse — huge volume, tolerates staleness), an order path (low volume, needs correctness), and a location path (courier firehose, needs throughput).

Deep Dive 1 — Courier matching & dispatch

"Assign the best courier to this order" is two problems stacked: a geospatial nearby-search (who's close?) and an optimization (who's best, accounting for the whole fleet?). Solve them in that order.

The nearby search. Finding available couriers near the restaurant is the identical 2-D range query from Uber and the proximity filter from Yelp. A B-tree on lat and lng separately can't prune in two dimensions at once. The fix: a spatial index that maps 2-D into a sortable 1-D key — GEOADD/GEOSEARCH over Redis (geohash encoded as a sorted-set score) answers "couriers within radius R" in milliseconds and absorbs the ping firehose. Only index available couriers so every candidate is actually dispatchable.

Dispatch strategyIdeaVerdict
Greedy nearestAssign the single closest free courier the instant the order is ready.~ Simple, low-latency, but globally suboptimal — strands far orders, ignores batching.
First-accept broadcastOffer to N nearby couriers; first to tap wins.~ Self-balancing, but noisy and can leave low-demand orders unclaimed.
Batched optimizationBriefly buffer orders + couriers, solve an assignment that minimizes total wait/idle miles, allow multi-pickup batching.✓ Higher fleet efficiency & fairness — DoorDash's real approach[1]; costs a few seconds of latency.

The optimization tradeoff. Greedy minimizes this order's wait but maximizes total miles across the fleet. Batched assignment (run every few seconds as a min-cost matching) trades a small latency hit for big gains in courier utilization, on-time rate, and the ability to batch two pickups from the same restaurant. The staff move is timing the dispatch to the food's readiness — assign so the courier arrives as the food comes up, not the moment the order is placed, minimizing both food-cold time and courier idle time.

★ Staff-level signal Senior candidates do greedy-nearest and stop. Staff candidates name dispatch as a global optimization under a latency budget: you buffer for a few seconds, solve a min-cost assignment over the current order/courier set, and tune the objective (wait time vs. miles vs. batching) to the business. Crucially, you tie assignment to predicted food-ready time, not order-placed time — recognizing that the real objective is system throughput and on-time delivery, not minimizing one courier's distance. That reframe is the whole signal.

Deep Dive 2 — Order state machine & the fulfillment saga

An order touches three systems that can each fail independently: payment (external PSP), the restaurant (may reject), and the courier (may not exist nearby). A single ACID transaction can't span them — they're separate services with separate datastores. This is the canonical saga from Lesson 10 — Common Patterns: a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating action if a later step fails.

ApproachMechanismVerdict
One distributed (2PC) txnLock payment + restaurant + courier, commit together.✗ Cross-service locks; the external PSP can't enlist; poor availability.
Choreographed sagaEach service emits events; others react via a bus.~ Decoupled, but the global flow is implicit and hard to reason about / debug.
Orchestrated sagaOrder Service drives steps explicitly; a durable workflow (Temporal/Step Functions) owns timeouts + compensation.✓ Explicit state, crash-safe resume, clear compensations — the staff pick.

The state machine. Model the order as explicit states — PENDING → PAID → CONFIRMED → DISPATCHED → PICKED_UP → DELIVERED, plus terminal CANCELLED/REFUNDED. Transitions are the only legal way to mutate an order; persist each one durably so the order survives any crash. Compensations: restaurant rejects after charge → refund payment, mark CANCELLED; no courier found in time → refund or re-queue. Make every external call idempotent (an idempotency key on the charge) so a retry after a timeout never double-charges[1].

★ Staff-level signal The junior answer wraps everything in "a transaction." The staff answer states plainly: you cannot 2PC across an external payment processor and a restaurant tablet, so the order is a saga of idempotent local steps with explicit compensations, driven by a durable orchestrator that survives crashes mid-flow. Pointing out that the charge needs an idempotency key — so a retried POST /orders after a network blip doesn't bill the customer twice — is the failure-mode judgment that separates a correct design from a plausible-looking one.

Deep Dive 3 — Serving menus at very high read scale

Menus are the ~50k-QPS hot path, yet they're read-mostly (a restaurant edits a menu a few times a day) and naturally cacheable. The discipline: never do a live relational join across menu/item/price tables on the request path. (Caching tiers come from Lesson 10 — Common Patterns.)

Read-scaling stack, cheapest layer first:
  • CDN at the edge. A rendered menu is a near-static document keyed by (restaurantId, version). Serve it from a CDN POP near the user — most menu reads never reach your origin at all.
  • Redis cache at origin. Cache misses hit Redis (the precomputed menu blob), not the SQL primary. TTL bounds staleness; an edit bumps the version and invalidates.
  • Read replicas. Replica reads absorb the rare cache miss so the primary is reserved for writes.
  • Denormalized blob. Store the menu as one precomputed JSON document, not a join — assembling it once on write beats assembling it 50k times/sec on read.

Invalidation. The interview gotcha is freshness: when a restaurant 86's a dish, the cached menu is stale. Fix with version-bumped keys — a menu edit writes a new version and the client/CDN fetches it; the old blob ages out by TTL. Per-item availability that changes fast (sold-out) can be overlaid from a small, separately-cached availability map rather than re-rendering the whole menu. Browse/list reads near a location reuse the same proximity prune as Gopuff.

Deep Dive 4 — Live order & courier tracking

After dispatch, the customer's phone needs the courier's moving position and the order's status changes — a server-push problem identical to the realtime pattern in Lesson 10 and the live trip streaming in Uber. Polling wastes battery and lags; use a persistent connection.

OptionFit for live tracking
Short polling✗ Per-request overhead, laggy, drains battery at a few-second cadence.
SSE (server→client)✓ One-way push fits customer tracking well; simpler than WebSockets.
WebSocket✓ Full-duplex — needed where the courier app also streams up; one primitive for both.

The Connection Service holds sockets keyed by orderId. The courier's location ping enters via the Location Service (updating the geo index); for an active delivery it's also relayed to the customer's socket, and status transitions from the Order Service are pushed the same way. The connection tier is a stateless fan-out/relay layer — keep the socket map in Redis so any node can route, and scale it independently of the order path. On disconnect the client reconnects and resumes; brief position gaps are acceptable, so this path favors availability over perfect ordering.

Check yourself — retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

1. Why is courier dispatch modeled as a batched optimization rather than greedy-nearest?

Greedy is locally optimal but globally wasteful; a buffered min-cost assignment trades seconds of latency for fleet efficiency, on-time rate, and batching.

2. Why is the order fulfillment flow a saga instead of one ACID transaction?

You can't 2PC across an external PSP and a restaurant tablet; the order becomes idempotent local steps with explicit compensations, driven by a durable orchestrator.

3. Why can the high-volume menu read path be served from a CDN and cache?

Menus are read ~100× more than they're written; precompute a versioned denormalized blob and serve it from CDN/Redis, invalidating by version bump.

4. Why must the payment charge in the order saga be idempotent?

Network timeouts trigger retries; an idempotency key on the charge ensures a re-sent POST /orders bills the customer exactly once.
